§1 — One pattern, many disguises

Every construction so far was a cone in denial

Look back at Lesson 6 with suspicious eyes. A terminal object: an object such that everything maps to it, uniquely. A product: an object with arrows to A and B, such that anything else with arrows to A and B factors through it, uniquely. Both times the recipe was the same: fix some target configuration, consider objects equipped with arrows to that configuration, and crown the best one. The only thing that changed was the shape of the configuration — nothing at all, or two isolated dots.

We have had the vocabulary for “shape” since Lesson 2. A diagram in 𝒞 of shape 𝒥 is just a functor D : 𝒥 → 𝒞, where 𝒥 is a small index category: its objects mark dots and its arrows mark which connecting arrows the diagram must supply. Lesson 2’s Exercise 6 already proved the first instance — functors out of 𝟚 are exactly arrows — and Lesson 3 showed that a natural transformation between two such diagrams is a commuting square. This lesson is where that machinery stops being an aside and becomes the main act.

The plan: define a cone over a diagram (§2), define the limit as the best cone, and then get the uniqueness theorem for every limit at once by a one-line reduction to Lesson 6. Then we tour the shapes (§3): the two we know, plus two new ones — equalizers and pullbacks — that turn out to be solution sets and database joins. Duality flips everything into colimits (§4). §5 then computes the limit of an arbitrary diagram, §6 asks which functors respect limits, and §7 does everything by hand — with an optional lab in which a single Python function computes the whole zoo.

§2 — Cones & limits

The best vantage point over a diagram

Definition · Cone

Let D : 𝒥 → 𝒞 be a diagram. A cone over D is an object X (the apex) together with one arrow xj : X → D(j) for each object j of 𝒥 (the legs), such that every triangle commutes: for every arrow a : i → j in 𝒥,

D(a) ∘ xi = xj.

That is the hero picture: an apex, a leg to every object of the diagram, and no way to cheat — going directly to D(j) must agree with going via any D(i). Lesson 2’s Exercise 8 planted a flag we can now collect: a cone with apex X is precisely a natural transformation ΔX ⇒ D from the constant functor at X. The legs are the components, and the cone condition is naturality — check it against the square, remembering that ΔX sends every arrow to idX.

Cones over a fixed D form a category of their own, Cone(D): an arrow from cone (X, x) to cone (L, λ) is an arrow m : X → L of 𝒞 that is compatible with all the legs, λj ∘ m = xj for every j. (Identities and composites are inherited from 𝒞; checking the axioms is Exercise 3.) With that category in hand, the central definition of the lesson costs four words:

Definition · Limit

A limit of D is a terminal object of Cone(D).

Unwound: a cone (lim D, λ) such that every cone (X, x) factors through it via exactly one arrow m : X → lim D with λj ∘ m = xj for all j.

Theorem · Uniqueness, once and for all

Any two limits of the same diagram are isomorphic via a unique cone-compatible isomorphism.

Proof. A limit is a terminal object in Cone(D), and Lesson 6 §2 proved that terminal objects are unique up to unique isomorphism — in any category. Apply that theorem to Cone(D). ∎

This is the promised payoff. In Lesson 6 we proved uniqueness for terminal objects, then re-proved it by hand for products (Exercise 5) and noted it dualizes. Now every limit of every shape — including every construction in the rest of this lesson — inherits the theorem from one four-sentence proof, applied in a cleverly chosen category. Building the right category and citing an old theorem inside it is one of the field’s signature moves; this is its first full appearance.

§3 — Shapes and their limits

Change the index category, change the construction

Rows one and two are Lesson 6 in new clothes. Over the empty diagram, a cone is just an apex — no legs, no conditions — so the terminal cone is the terminal object. Over two isolated dots picking out A and B, a cone is an object with arrows to each, the triangles are vacuous, and the terminal cone is exactly the product with its projections. (Exercise 1 has you verify both unwindings carefully.) The new rows are where the shape starts doing real work.

The parallel pair: equalizers

Let 𝒥 be two objects with two parallel arrows between them, and let the diagram pick out f, g : A → B. A cone is an apex X with legs to A and B — but the triangle conditions force the B-leg to equal both f ∘ xA and g ∘ xA. So a cone is really just an arrow x : X → A with f ∘ x = g ∘ x — an arrow that cannot tell f and g apart. The limit, written e : E → A and called the equalizer, is the universal such arrow.

In Set the equalizer is the solution set: everything in A where the equation f(a) = g(a) holds, sitting inside A via the inclusion map. Solving equations — the oldest activity in mathematics — is computing a limit over the second-smallest interesting shape. And the universal property captures precisely what a solution set is for: any way of landing in A that satisfies the equation lands, in exactly one way, in the solutions.

The cospan: pullbacks

Now let 𝒥 be the cospan • → • ← •, so the diagram is a pair of arrows with a common target: f : A → Z and g : B → Z. A cone is an apex with arrows to A and B that agree upon arrival in Z (the leg to Z is forced, just as with the equalizer). The limit is the pullback A ×Z B — the product of A and B, constrained by the shared map to Z.

Pullbacks are everywhere once you know the shape. Two subsets included in a common set pull back to their intersection. A function f : A → B pulled back along a subset inclusion S ↪ B gives the inverse image f−1(S) (Exercise 5). And two database tables sharing a key column pull back to their join: the pairs of rows that agree on the key — which is exactly what §7 computes. When the maps to Z carry the meaning “this row’s foreign key,” the pullback carries the meaning “match them up.”

Monomorphisms are pullbacks

Lesson 1 defined a monomorphism by cancellation. Pullbacks give a second description, in which the cancellation property becomes a universal property — and with it, monomorphisms become something that limit-preserving functors must respect.

Proposition · Monic means a trivial pullback

An arrow m : A → B is monic if and only if the square with m on two sides and identities on the other two —

m ∘ idA = m ∘ idA

— is a pullback square; that is, if and only if A, with the two legs idA and idA, is a pullback of m along itself.

Proof. A cone over the cospan A → B ← A (both arrows m) is a pair h, k : X → A with m ∘ h = m ∘ k. The square is a pullback exactly when every such pair factors uniquely through (A, idA, idA): there is a unique u : X → A with idA ∘ u = h and idA ∘ u = k. Such a u exists exactly when h = k, and is then unique. So the square is a pullback if and only if m ∘ h = m ∘ k always forces h = k — which is the definition of monic. ∎

In Set the pullback of m along itself is the set of pairs (a, a′) with m(a) = m(a′), and the proposition says: m is injective exactly when that set contains only the pairs (a, a). A second fact of the same kind — the pullback of a monomorphism along any arrow is again a monomorphism — is Exercise 13; in Set it says that the inverse image of a subset is a subset.

§4 — Colimits

Reverse the legs, glue instead of carve

Duality now does a lesson’s worth of work in a paragraph. A cocone under a diagram D is an apex X with legs D(j) → X out of the diagram, commuting with the diagram’s arrows; equivalently, a natural transformation D ⇒ ΔX. A colimit is an initial cocone — the universal way to fuse the diagram into a single object — and it is unique up to unique isomorphism because Lesson 6’s theorem dualizes (or directly: apply the terminal-object theorem in Cocone(D)op).

The zoo dualizes row by row. The colimit of the empty diagram is the initial object; of two dots, the coproduct. The dual of the equalizer is the coequalizer of f, g : A → B: in Set, the quotient of B by the equivalence relation generated by f(a) ∼ g(a) — where equalizers solve equations, coequalizers impose them, which is how quotient constructions throughout mathematics arise. The dual of the pullback is the pushout: the universal way to glue A and B along a shared part Z, as when two line segments are joined at an endpoint, or two sets are united over their intersection (Exercise 7). Limits carve out; colimits glue together.

Aside · Two shapes suffice

A category with all binary products, a terminal object, and all equalizers automatically has every finite limit — pullbacks, and limits over any finite shape whatsoever, can be assembled from those parts. Exercise 8 walks through the first case: a pullback is an equalizer of two maps out of a product. §5 proves the general statement: with products of arbitrary families in place of binary ones, the same two constructions give every limit — which is how one shows that Set has all small limits.

A coequalizer and a pushout, computed

Colimits in Set are built by a two-step recipe — put the pieces side by side, then glue — and it is worth doing one of each by hand.

A coequalizer. Let A = {1, 2} and B = {a, b, c, d}, with f(1) = a, f(2) = c and g(1) = b, g(2) = c. The coequalizer is the quotient of B in which f(x) and g(x) are identified for each x ∈ A. From x = 1: a ∼ b. From x = 2: c ∼ c, which says nothing. So the coequalizer is the three-element set

{ {a, b}, {c}, {d} },

with the map q : B → Q sending each element to its class. Its universal property: a function h out of B with h ∘ f = h ∘ g — that is, with h(a) = h(b) — factors through q in exactly one way. Quotient sets, quotient groups and quotient spaces are all coequalizers; for instance ℤ/n is the coequalizer, in the category of abelian groups, of multiplication by n and the zero map ℤ → ℤ.

A pushout. Take two copies of the interval [0, 1] and a one-point set Z = {∗}, mapped to the right end 1 of the first interval and the left end 0 of the second. The pushout is formed by putting the two intervals side by side and then identifying those two endpoints: the result is a single interval of length two. Gluing spaces along a common part — two segments at a point, two discs along their rims to make a sphere — is the meaning of a pushout in topology, and Exercise 7 shows that the union of two sets is their pushout over the intersection.

§5 — Limits in general

One formula for sets, one recipe for everything

So far each shape was handled separately. This section computes the limit of an arbitrary diagram, first in Set by an explicit formula and then in any category by a recipe that needs only two of the shapes in the table.

The limit of any diagram of sets

Let D : 𝒥 → Set be a diagram, with 𝒥 small. A cone from a one-element set picks one element xj from each set D(j), in such a way that the functions of the diagram carry these elements to one another. Collect all such choices:

Theorem · Limits in Set

The set of compatible families

L = { (xj) : xj ∈ D(j) for each object j,

and D(a)(xi) = xj for each arrow a : i → j },

with the legs λj : L → D(j) that read off the j-th entry, is a limit of D. In particular Set has all small limits.

Proof. The legs form a cone: for a : i → j, applying D(a) to the i-th entry of a compatible family gives its j-th entry, which is what D(a) ∘ λi = λj says. Now let (X, x) be any cone, with legs xj : X → D(j). A function m : X → L with λj ∘ m = xj for all j must send each p ∈ X to the family whose j-th entry is xj(p) — so there is at most one such m. And that family is compatible, because the cone condition D(a) ∘ xi = xj says so; hence m exists. ∎

Every limit in the table of §3 is this formula for a particular shape. No arrows: all families, the product. A parallel pair: families (x, y) with f(x) = y = g(x), which amounts to the solution set of f(x) = g(x). A cospan: families (a, b, z) with f(a) = z = g(b), the pairs that agree in Z. And for a shape you have not seen — a tower ⋯ → X2 → X1 → X0 — the limit is the set of infinite sequences (x0, x1, x2, …) in which each term is the image of the next. If the maps are inclusions of nested subsets, that is their intersection.

Every limit from products and equalizers

The formula has two ingredients: form all families (a product), then impose equations (an equalizer). The same two steps work in any category.

Theorem · Products and equalizers suffice

If a category has all (small) products and all equalizers, then it has all (small) limits. If it has finite products — binary products and a terminal object — and equalizers, then it has all finite limits.

Proof. Let D : 𝒥 → 𝒞 be a diagram. Form two products: P, the product of the objects D(j) over all objects j of 𝒥; and Q, the product of the objects D(j) over all arrows a : i → j of 𝒥, one factor D(j) for each arrow, where j is the target of a. An arrow into Q is determined by its components, one for each a. Define two arrows s, t : P → Q by saying what their a-th components are:

sa = πj    and    ta = D(a) ∘ πi     for a : i → j.

Now let x : X → P be any arrow, with components xj = πj ∘ x. The a-th component of s ∘ x is xj, and that of t ∘ x is D(a) ∘ xi. So s ∘ x = t ∘ x holds exactly when D(a) ∘ xi = xj for every arrow a — exactly when the family (xj) is a cone over D. Cones over D are thus the same thing as arrows into P that equalize s and t, and a universal one is the same thing as an equalizer of s and t. So the equalizer e : E → P, with legs πj ∘ e, is a limit of D. If 𝒥 is finite, both products are finite. ∎

This is the precise form of the slogan “two shapes suffice.” To prove that a category has all limits you exhibit products and equalizers and stop — and by duality, coproducts and coequalizers give all colimits.

Limits in an order

In a preorder a cone over a diagram is an element below every object of the diagram; the arrows of the diagram impose no further condition, since every triangle commutes. So the limit of any diagram is the meet — the greatest lower bound — of the elements it involves, and the colimit is their join. A preorder has all limits exactly when every set of elements has a meet. The power set of a set, ordered by inclusion, is the standard example: the limit of a family of subsets is their intersection, the colimit their union.

§6 — Preserving limits

Maps into a limit are cones

When does a functor carry limits to limits?

Definition · Preservation

A functor F : 𝒞 → 𝒟 preserves the limit of a diagram D if, whenever (L, λ) is a limit cone of D, the cone (F L, F λ) — with legs F(λj) — is a limit cone of the diagram F ∘ D. Preservation of colimits is defined dually.

Any functor sends cones to cones, because it preserves the commuting triangles. The question is whether it sends the universal cone to a universal one. Many functors do not: the forgetful functor Grp → Set does not preserve coproducts, since the underlying set of the free product of two groups is far larger than the disjoint union of their underlying sets. But one family of functors always does.

Theorem · Hom functors preserve limits

For every object X of a locally small category 𝒞, the functor 𝒞(X, −) : 𝒞 → Set preserves every limit that exists in 𝒞. In formulas: if D has a limit, then

𝒞(X, lim D) ≅ lim 𝒞(X, D(j)),

where the right-hand limit is taken in Set.

Proof. By the formula of §5, an element of the limit on the right is a compatible family of elements xj ∈ 𝒞(X, D(j)): a family of arrows xj : X → D(j) with D(a) ∘ xi = xj for every arrow a of the shape. That is exactly a cone over D with apex X. And the universal property of lim D says that cones with apex X correspond one-to-one to arrows X → lim D, a cone corresponding to its mediating arrow m, from which it is recovered as xj = λj ∘ m. So the function sending m to the family (λj ∘ m) is a bijection from 𝒞(X, lim D) to the limit of the sets 𝒞(X, D(j)), and it is the function induced by the legs 𝒞(X, λj). ∎

The theorem deserves to be read as a slogan: an arrow into a limit is the same thing as a cone. For a product it says 𝒞(X, A × B) ≅ 𝒞(X, A) × 𝒞(X, B) — an arrow into a product is a pair of arrows. For a pullback: an arrow into A ×Z B is a pair of arrows agreeing in Z. Dually, an arrow out of a colimit is the same thing as a cocone, so the contravariant hom functors turn colimits into limits:

𝒞(colim D, X) ≅ lim 𝒞(D(j), X).

For a coproduct this reads 𝒞(A + B, X) ≅ 𝒞(A, X) × 𝒞(B, X). Counted in Set, these two isomorphisms are the laws of exponents met in Lesson 6. The theorem will be used twice more: Lesson 8 derives from it the fact that right adjoints preserve limits, and Lesson 9 shows that it characterises limits — an object is the limit of D exactly when its arrows from every X are the cones.

§7 — Limits by hand

List the cones, find the best one

In a small category the limit of a diagram can be found by the method of Lesson 6: list the candidates — here, the cones — and look for the one that every other factors through, exactly once. Four computations.

Three shapes in the diamond

Work in the divisibility order on {1, 2, 3, 6}. Because the category is thin, a cone over a diagram is just an element dividing every object of the diagram (§5), and a cone factors through another when its apex divides the other’s.

The cospan gives the same answer as the two dots: a cone needs a leg to 6 as well, but anything dividing 2 divides 6, so the extra object adds no condition. In a thin category a pullback is always just a meet. Reversing the arrows, the cocones under the two dots 2, 3 are the common multiples — only 6 — so the colimit, the coproduct, is 6.

An equalizer and a pullback of sets

Equalizer. Let f(x) = x2 and g(x) = x + 2 on the set {−3, −2, …, 3}. The equalizer is the set of solutions of x2 = x + 2, that is, of (x − 2)(x + 1) = 0: the subset {−1, 2}, with its inclusion. Check the universal property on one cone: the function h from a two-element set {p, q} with h(p) = 2 and h(q) = 2 satisfies f ∘ h = g ∘ h, and it factors through the inclusion of {−1, 2} in exactly one way.

Pullback. Take a table of employees with their departments, and a table of departments with their buildings:

employeedeptdeptbuilding
AliceengengBuilding 1
BobsalessalesBuilding 2
Caroleng

Let E be the set of rows of the first table and B the set of rows of the second, with the functions E → K and B → K that read off the department. The pullback E ×K B is the set of pairs of rows with the same department: (Alice, eng–Building 1), (Bob, sales–Building 2), (Carol, eng–Building 1). Three pairs out of a possible six. That is the join of the two tables on the department column: the query a database runs to answer “who works in which building” is the computation of a limit.

A limit in a category that is not thin

In a thin category equalizers are invisible, since parallel arrows are equal. So here is the smallest category in which one can be seen. The fork has three objects E, A, B, and besides the identities four arrows:

e : E → A,     f, g : A → B,     w : E → B,    with  f ∘ e = w = g ∘ e.

The arrows f and g are different. What are the cones over the parallel pair (f, g)? A cone is an arrow x into A with f ∘ x = g ∘ x. The arrows into A are idA and e. For idA: f ≠ g, so it is not a cone. For e: f ∘ e = g ∘ e, so it is. There is exactly one cone, (E, e), and it factors through itself only by idE — the only arrow E → E. So e is the equalizer of f and g. Seven arrows are enough to exhibit a genuine equalizer.

Hom functors, counted

§6 says that arrows into a limit are cones. In the diamond, take X = 1 and the two-dot diagram 2, 3 with limit 1: there is one arrow 1 → 1, and one cone with apex 1 (the pair 1 | 2, 1 | 3). In Set, take the pullback P of the tables above and let X be a one-element set: arrows X → P are the 3 elements of P, and cones with apex X are the pairs (employee row, building row) with matching department — the same 3. For a two-element X both counts are 32 = 9.

Optional · PythonOne limit finder for every shape

Lesson 6’s code had one finder per construction — terminal_objects, find_products, find_coproducts. This lesson’s definitions say those should all be a single function, parameterized by a diagram. A diagram is a Functor from Lesson 2; a cone is an apex plus a legs dictionary; and the limit is found by checking the terminal-cone property by enumeration. The whole finder is shorter than any two of Lesson 6’s specialists combined:

setup.py — carried over from Lessons 1, 2 and 6
from itertools import product

class FiniteCategory:
    """objects, arrows (name -> (dom, cod)), a composition table,
       and a chosen identity arrow for each object."""
    def __init__(self, objects, arrows, comp, ident):
        self.obj, self.ar = set(objects), arrows
        self.comp, self.id = comp, ident

    def dom(self, a): return self.ar[a][0]
    def cod(self, a): return self.ar[a][1]
    def composable(self, g, f): return self.dom(g) == self.cod(f)

    def check(self):
        # 1. each identity is typed X -> X
        for X in self.obj:
            assert self.ar[self.id[X]] == (X, X)
        # 2. every composable pair has a composite, correctly typed
        for g, f in product(self.ar, repeat=2):
            if self.composable(g, f):
                gf = self.comp[(g, f)]
                assert self.ar[gf] == (self.dom(f), self.cod(g))
        # 3. identity law on both sides
        for f in self.ar:
            X, Y = self.ar[f]
            assert self.comp[(self.id[Y], f)] == f
            assert self.comp[(f, self.id[X])] == f
        # 4. associativity
        for h, g, f in product(self.ar, repeat=3):
            if self.composable(h, g) and self.composable(g, f):
                assert self.comp[(h, self.comp[(g, f)])] == \
                       self.comp[(self.comp[(h, g)], f)]
        return True

class Functor:
    """F : C -> D, given by a map on objects and a map on arrows."""
    def __init__(self, C, D, on_obj, on_arr):
        self.C, self.D, self.on_obj, self.on_arr = C, D, on_obj, on_arr

    def check(self):
        C, D, Fo, Fa = self.C, self.D, self.on_obj, self.on_arr
        for f in C.ar:                        # typing: F f : F X -> F Y
            X, Y = C.ar[f]
            assert D.ar[Fa[f]] == (Fo[X], Fo[Y])
        for X in C.obj:                       # preserves identities
            assert Fa[C.id[X]] == D.id[Fo[X]]
        for g, f in product(C.ar, repeat=2):  # preserves composition
            if C.composable(g, f):
                assert Fa[C.comp[(g, f)]] == D.comp[(Fa[g], Fa[f])]
        return True

def arrows_between(C, X, Y):
    "all arrows X -> Y of the finite category C"
    return [a for a, (d, c) in C.ar.items() if d == X and c == Y]

limits.py — the general limit finder
def cones(C, D):
    "All cones over the diagram D : J -> C, as (apex, legs) pairs."
    J, out = D.C, []
    Jobjs = sorted(J.obj, key=str)
    for X in C.obj:
        legsets = [arrows_between(C, X, D.on_obj[j]) for j in Jobjs]
        for combo in cartesian(*legsets):
            legs = dict(zip(Jobjs, combo))
            # every triangle commutes:  D(a) . leg_i == leg_j
            if all(C.comp[(D.on_arr[a], legs[i])] == legs[j]
                   for a, (i, j) in J.ar.items()):
                out.append((X, legs))
    return out

def find_limits(C, D):
    "Limit = the cone through which every cone factors exactly once."
    cs = cones(C, D)
    return [(apex, legs) for apex, legs in cs
            if all(len([m for m in arrows_between(C, X, apex)
                        if all(C.comp[(legs[j], m)] == l[j]
                               for j in legs)]) == 1
                   for X, l in cs)]

Its dual needs no new ideas: reverse the direction of every leg and of every mediating arrow, and flip each composite.

colimits.py — the arrow-reversed twin
def cocones(C, D):
    "All cocones under D, as (apex, legs) pairs: legs run D(j) -> apex."
    J, out = D.C, []
    Jobjs = sorted(J.obj, key=str)
    for X in C.obj:
        legsets = [arrows_between(C, D.on_obj[j], X) for j in Jobjs]
        for combo in cartesian(*legsets):
            legs = dict(zip(Jobjs, combo))
            # every triangle commutes:  leg_j . D(a) == leg_i
            if all(C.comp[(legs[j], D.on_arr[a])] == legs[i]
                   for a, (i, j) in J.ar.items()):
                out.append((X, legs))
    return out

def find_colimits(C, D):
    "Colimit = the cocone that factors through every cocone exactly once."

            if all(len([m for m in arrows_between(C, apex, X)
                        if all(C.comp[(m, legs[j])] == l[j]
                               for j in legs)]) == 1
                   for X, l in cs)]

Going deeper

The pasting lemma

Pullbacks compose in a way worth knowing by name. Draw two commuting squares side by side, sharing a vertical edge — a wide rectangle subdivided into a left square and a right square. The pasting lemma says: if the right square is a pullback, then the outer rectangle is a pullback if and only if the left square is.

This is the working tool behind many “obvious” manipulations: computing an iterated inverse image f−1(g−1(S)) in one step, or chaining two joins on shared keys and knowing the result is itself a join. Categorically it says pullbacks paste like tiles — the same gluing behavior that naturality squares showed in Lesson 3, Exercise 3, now upgraded from “commuting” to “universal.” Exercise 10 proves one direction and Exercise 16 the other; each proof is a few universal properties taking turns, in the style this course has been rehearsing since Lesson 6.

Exercise 1warm-up

Carefully unwind the definition of a limit over the two smallest shapes:

  1. The empty index category. What is a cone over the empty diagram? What does the terminal-cone condition say? Conclude that the limit is exactly a terminal object of 𝒞.
  2. The discrete two-object index category (two dots, identity arrows only). Show that a cone is exactly “an object with an arrow to A and an arrow to B,” and that the limit condition reproduces, word for word, Lesson 6’s definition of the product.
Show solution

(a) With no objects in 𝒥 there are no legs to give and no triangles to check, so a cone is just an apex: every object of 𝒞 is one, in exactly one way. A cone morphism (X) → (L) is any arrow X → L — the compatibility conditions are vacuous too. So Cone(D) is just 𝒞 itself, and its terminal object is a terminal object of 𝒞.

(b) The only arrows of 𝒥 are identities, and the triangle for idj reads D(idj) ∘ xj = xj — automatic, since functors preserve identities. So a cone is an unconstrained pair of arrows xA : X → A, xB : X → B. The terminal cone (P, π1, π2) must admit, from every such pair, exactly one m with π1 ∘ m = xA and π2 ∘ m = xB — which is Lesson 6’s product definition verbatim, with ⟨xA, xB⟩ = m.

Exercise 2warm-up

What is the limit of a diagram over the one-object, one-arrow index category 𝟙 — i.e. of a diagram that just picks out a single object A of 𝒞?

Guess first, then verify your guess against the universal property.

Show solution

The limit is A itself, with the identity as its single leg. A cone is any arrow x : X → A (the one triangle, over id, is automatic). The cone (A, idA) is terminal among these: given x : X → A, a mediating arrow m must satisfy idA ∘ m = x, forcing m = x — existence and uniqueness in one line.

So “the limit of a single object is that object”: reassuringly boring, and a useful sanity check that the machinery adds nothing when there is nothing to add. (Dually, the colimit over 𝟙 is also A.)

Exercise 3structure

§2 asserted that cones over a fixed diagram D form a category Cone(D). Verify the claim: say what the identity on a cone (X, x) is, show that the composite of two cone morphisms is a cone morphism, and explain where the category laws come from.

Show solution

The identity on (X, x) is idX, which is leg-compatible since xj ∘ idX = xj. For composites, suppose m : (X, x) → (Y, y) and n : (Y, y) → (L, λ) are cone morphisms, so yj ∘ m = xj and λj ∘ n = yj for all j. Then λj ∘ (n ∘ m) = (λj ∘ n) ∘ m = yj ∘ m = xj — compatible again, using associativity once per step.

Associativity and the identity laws hold because composition in Cone(D) is composition in 𝒞, merely restricted to arrows that happen to respect legs. Categories whose arrows are “arrows of 𝒞 satisfying extra conditions” inherit the laws for free — the same phenomenon as the injective-functions category of Lesson 1, Exercise 4.

Exercise 4examples

Compute two equalizers in Set, concretely:

  1. The equalizer of f(x) = x³ and g(x) = x on the set X = {−2, −1, 0, 1, 2}.
  2. The equalizer of any f : A → B with itself. What does the answer say, in slogan form, about what equalizers measure?
Show solution

(a) Solve x³ = x, i.e. x(x−1)(x+1) = 0: the equalizer is {−1, 0, 1}, included into X. (One comprehension in Python: [x for x in X if x**3 == x].)

(b) The condition f(a) = f(a) holds everywhere, so the equalizer of f with itself is all of A, with e = idA (up to the usual unique isomorphism). Slogan: an equalizer measures disagreement — it is the part of the domain on which two arrows cannot be told apart, and two copies of the same arrow disagree nowhere. This also explains why equalizers are invisible in thin categories: parallel arrows are already equal there, so every equalizer is trivial — and why §7 demonstrates equalizers in Set rather than in the divisibility poset.

Exercise 5structure

Inverse images are pullbacks. Let f : A → B be a function and S ⊆ B a subset, with inclusion map i : S ↪ B.

Compute the pullback A ×B S of f and i as a set of pairs, and exhibit a bijection between it and the inverse image f−1(S) = { a ∈ A : f(a) ∈ S }. Try it concretely with A = {1,2,3,4}, f(a) = a mod 2, B = {0,1}, S = {0}.

Show solution

The pullback is { (a, s) : f(a) = i(s) } = { (a, s) : f(a) = s, s ∈ S }. Since the second coordinate is forced to equal f(a), the map (a, s) ↦ a is a bijection onto { a : f(a) ∈ S } = f−1(S), with inverse a ↦ (a, f(a)). Concretely: the pairs are {(2, 0), (4, 0)}, and the bijection lands on f−1({0}) = {2, 4} — the even elements.

So “take the preimage” is “pull back along an inclusion.” This is where the name comes from: the subset S of the codomain is pulled back along f to a subset of the domain. Filtering a dataset by a condition on a derived column is this construction, performed daily.

Exercise 6arrows-only

An arrow m : X → Y is a monomorphism (is monic) if it is cancellable on the left: whenever m ∘ h = m ∘ k for arrows h, k : W → X, it follows that h = k. (In Set, monic = injective.)

Prove that every equalizer arrow is monic: if e : E → A is the equalizer of f, g : A → B, and e ∘ h = e ∘ k, then h = k. Hint: name the common composite x = e ∘ h, check that x is a cone, and let uniqueness of mediators finish.

Show solution

Let x = e ∘ h = e ∘ k : W → A. It equalizes the pair: f ∘ x = (f ∘ e) ∘ h = (g ∘ e) ∘ h = g ∘ x, using f ∘ e = g ∘ e (the equalizer’s own fork) and associativity. So x is a cone over the parallel pair, and the universal property says there is exactly one arrow m : W → E with e ∘ m = x.

But two such arrows are staring at us: h and k both satisfy e ∘ (–) = x — that is how x was defined. Uniqueness forces h = k. ∎ This is the cleanest specimen yet of the Lesson-6 proof pattern: to show two arrows are equal, exhibit both as mediators for the same cone and let “exactly one” do the work. In Set it recovers the obvious fact that a subset inclusion is injective — but the proof never looked at an element, so it holds in every category with equalizers.

Exercise 7duality

Dualize §3’s pullback discussion:

  1. State the definition of the pushout of a span A ← Z → B by reversing every arrow in the pullback’s universal property.
  2. In Set, let Z = A ∩ B with the two inclusions into A = {1, 2} and B = {2, 3}. Compute the pushout and identify it with a familiar set. What happened to the two copies of the element 2?
Show solution

(a) Given f : Z → A and g : Z → B, the pushout is an object A +Z B with arrows j1 : A → A +Z B and j2 : B → A +Z B satisfying j1 ∘ f = j2 ∘ g, such that for any X with p : A → X, q : B → X and p ∘ f = q ∘ g, there is exactly one m : A +Z B → X with m ∘ j1 = p and m ∘ j2 = q.

(b) Start with the coproduct — the disjoint union with tags, {('L',1), ('L',2), ('R',2), ('R',3)} from Lesson 6 — and then impose the equation j1(f(z)) = j2(g(z)) for each z ∈ Z = {2}: glue ('L',2) to ('R',2). The result has three points and is in bijection with the plain union {1, 2, 3}. Where Lesson 6’s coproduct kept the two 2’s apart, the pushout is precisely the instruction to merge them — union is coproduct relative to the intersection. (And the recipe “coproduct, then coequalize” is the dual of the aside in §4: pushouts from coproducts and coequalizers.)

Exercise 8think

Prove the first case of §4’s amber aside: a category with binary products and equalizers has all pullbacks.

Given f : A → Z and g : B → Z, form the product A × B and the two composites f ∘ π1, g ∘ π2 : A × B → Z. Show that their equalizer e : E → A × B, equipped with the legs π1 ∘ e and π2 ∘ e, satisfies the pullback’s universal property. (Sanity-check the construction in Set first: what set does it build?)

Show solution

In Set the recipe builds { (a,b) ∈ A × B : f(a) = g(b) } — exactly the pullback of §3 — which is the right intuition to carry into the general proof.

The square commutes: f ∘ (π1 ∘ e) = (f ∘ π1) ∘ e = (g ∘ π2) ∘ e = g ∘ (π2 ∘ e), the middle step being the equalizer’s fork. Universality: take any X with p : X → A, q : X → B, f ∘ p = g ∘ q. The product gives ⟨p, q⟩ : X → A × B, and it equalizes the pair — (f ∘ π1) ∘ ⟨p,q⟩ = f ∘ p = g ∘ q = (g ∘ π2) ∘ ⟨p,q⟩ — so it factors through e via a unique m : X → E, and the legs check out: (π1 ∘ e) ∘ m = π1 ∘ ⟨p,q⟩ = p, likewise for q.

Uniqueness of m: any competitor m′ with the same leg equations has e ∘ m′ = ⟨p, q⟩ — because e ∘ m′ hits p and q under the projections, and the product’s uniqueness clause pins such an arrow down — and then Exercise 6 (equalizer arrows are monic) forces m′ = m. Three universal properties, each contributing its uniqueness exactly where needed: this is what “assembling limits from parts” looks like in practice.

Exercise 10think

Set up the pasting lemma’s notation: objects X, Y, Z across the top, A, B, C across the bottom, vertical arrows x, y, z downward, horizontal arrows completing a left square (X, Y, A, B) and a right square (Y, Z, B, C), both commuting. Assume the right square is a pullback.

Prove one direction: if the left square is a pullback, the outer rectangle is. (Take a cone over the outer cospan — T → A and T → Z agreeing in C — and factor it in two stages, right square first.)

Show solution

Let a : T → A and t : T → Z agree in C. Stage one: composing a across the bottom gives T → B, which together with t forms a cone over the right cospan (they agree in C by assumption); the right pullback yields a unique u : T → Y compatible with both. Stage two: now a and u form a cone over the left cospan (compatibility from stage one is exactly agreement in B); the left pullback yields a unique v : T → X, and chasing the two triangles shows v is compatible with the outer rectangle’s legs a and t.

Uniqueness: a competitor v′ for the outer rectangle composes to a T → Y that satisfies stage one’s conditions, so it equals u by the right square’s uniqueness; then v′ satisfies stage two’s conditions and equals v by the left square’s. Existence built in two stages, uniqueness collapsed in two stages — Lesson 6’s division of labor, run twice in sequence. (The converse direction, outer-implies-left, is the same game with the roles of the cones rearranged.)

Exercise 13arrows-only

Monomorphisms are stable under pullback. Let the square with p1 : P → A, p2 : P → B, f : A → Z and m : B → Z be a pullback, so f ∘ p1 = m ∘ p2, and suppose m is monic. Prove that p1 is monic.

Show solution

Write r : X → Y and s : Y → Z for the top arrows and u : A → B, v : B → C for the bottom ones. Take a cone over the left cospan: arrows a : T → A and b : T → Y with u ∘ a = y ∘ b.

Existence. The arrows a and s ∘ b : T → Z form a cone over the outer cospan, because v ∘ u ∘ a = v ∘ y ∘ b = z ∘ s ∘ b, using the right square. The outer pullback gives a unique m : T → X with x ∘ m = a and s ∘ r ∘ m = s ∘ b. We still need r ∘ m = b. Both r ∘ m and b are arrows T → Y; they agree after s (just shown), and they agree after y, since y ∘ r ∘ m = u ∘ x ∘ m = u ∘ a = y ∘ b. Arrows into the right pullback are determined by their composites with s and y, so r ∘ m = b.

Uniqueness. If m′ also satisfies x ∘ m′ = a and r ∘ m′ = b, then s ∘ r ∘ m′ = s ∘ b, so m′ is a mediating arrow for the same outer cone, and m′ = m.

The right square was used twice, and in two different ways: once as a commuting square, to produce the outer cone, and once as a pullback, to compare two arrows into Y. That second use is what makes this direction the subtler one.

Exercise 17examples

Limits in orders (§5).

  1. In the divisibility order on {1, 2, 3, 6}, what are the limit and the colimit of the diagram consisting of all four objects and all the arrows between them?
  2. Let 𝒞 be any preorder, and regard the identity functor 𝒞 → 𝒞 as a diagram: all the objects and all the arrows. Show that a limit of this diagram, if there is one, is a least element of 𝒞. What is a colimit?
  3. In the power set of {1, 2, 3} ordered by inclusion, compute the limit and colimit of the diagram consisting of the three subsets {1, 2}, {2, 3} and {2}.
Show solution

(a) The limit is the greatest element dividing all of 1, 2, 3, 6, namely 1. The colimit is the least common multiple of all of them, 6.

(b) A cone over the identity diagram is an element below every element of 𝒞 — a least element. Any two least elements are below each other, so each is a greatest one among them: the limit, if it exists, is a least element, that is, an initial object. Dually a colimit is a greatest element, a terminal object. (The same is true in every category, not only in preorders: a limit of the identity functor is an initial object. In a preorder it is easy to see.)

(c) Limit: the intersection {1, 2} ∩ {2, 3} ∩ {2} = {2}. Colimit: the union {1, 2, 3}. The inclusions {2} ⊆ {1, 2} and {2} ⊆ {2, 3} that are part of the diagram change nothing.

Exercise 18arrows-only

Two facts about equalizers, to go with Exercise 6. Let e : E → A be an equalizer of f, g : A → B.

  1. Show that f = g if and only if e is an isomorphism.
  2. Show that if e is epic, then e is an isomorphism.
Show solution

(a) If f = g, then idA is a cone (f ∘ id = g ∘ id) and it is universal: any x : X → A factors through idA as x itself, uniquely. So (A, idA) is an equalizer, and by uniqueness of limits e differs from idA by an isomorphism — so e is one. Conversely, if e is an isomorphism, then from f ∘ e = g ∘ e we get f = g by composing with e−1.

(b) From f ∘ e = g ∘ e and e epic, cancel e on the right: f = g. Now apply (a).

So an equalizer is always monic (Exercise 6), and it is epic only in the degenerate case. An arrow that is monic and epic need not be an isomorphism (Lesson 1) — but an equalizer that is epic must be. Monomorphisms that arise as equalizers are better behaved than monomorphisms in general; they are called regular.

Recap

What to carry forward

  1. A diagram is a functor D : 𝒥 → 𝒞 from an index category; a cone is an apex with commuting legs to it — equivalently, a natural transformation ΔX ⇒ D. The machinery of Lessons 2, 3 and 5, cashed in.
  2. A limit is a terminal object in the category of cones — so one application of Lesson 6’s theorem gives uniqueness up to unique isomorphism for every limit of every shape, forever.
  3. The shape is a dial: empty ↦ terminal object, two dots ↦ product, parallel pair ↦ equalizer (solution sets), cospan ↦ pullback (intersections, inverse images, joins).
  4. Colimits are the duals — initial cocones: coproducts, coequalizers (quotients), pushouts (gluing). Limits carve out; colimits glue together.
  5. Products and equalizers generate all limits (finite products and equalizers, all finite ones). In Set a limit is the set of compatible families; in an order it is a meet.
  6. Hom functors preserve limits: an arrow into a limit is the same thing as a cone, and an arrow out of a colimit is a cocone. An arrow is monic exactly when its pullback along itself is trivial.
  7. In a finite category the whole theory is one search — list the cones, find the best — with the shape as a parameter.
