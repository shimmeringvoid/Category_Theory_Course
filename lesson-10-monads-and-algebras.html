Monads & Algebras

Every adjunction, folded in half, becomes a monad: an endofunctor that knows how to flatten itself. Its laws are the reason effects compose, and its algebras are the structures it describes — beginning with the monoids of Lesson 1.

§1 — An adjunction, folded in half

What is left when the second category is forgotten

Lesson 8 built adjunctions F ⊣ U and kept the two categories separate. But nothing stops us composing the two functors into a single endofunctor T = U ∘ F on 𝒞 — for the free-monoid adjunction, T = List. What structure does T inherit from the adjunction it came from?

The simplest case shows what to expect. Between posets an adjunction is a Galois connection f ⊣ g, and Lesson 8’s last exercise looked at the composite c = g ∘ f. It is monotone, and it satisfies

x ≤ c(x)    and    c(c(x)) ≤ c(x).

An operation of this kind is a closure operator: the subgroup generated by a set of elements, the span of a set of vectors, the closure of a set of points. Each of these lives on one poset — the subsets of a group, of a vector space, of a space — and the second poset of the Galois connection is nowhere to be seen. Yet something of it survives. The elements with c(x) = x, the closed ones, are exactly the subgroups, the subspaces, the closed sets.

In a category that is not thin the same thing happens, with two changes. The two inequalities become two natural transformations, a unit η : Id ⇒ T and a multiplication μ : T ∘ T ⇒ T. And because parallel arrows may now differ, the two must satisfy equations. An endofunctor with this structure is a monad. You have used one since Lesson 2: for T = List the unit wraps an element as a one-element list, and the multiplication is flatten, which Lesson 3 checked to be natural and filed away for the end of the course. This is the end of the course.

The lesson has two halves. The first defines monads (§2), shows that every adjunction produces one (§3), and collects examples (§4). The second asks the converse question: does every monad come from an adjunction? It does, in two canonical ways. One is the Kleisli category (§5), whose arrows are “functions with an effect”; the monad laws turn out to be exactly what makes such functions composable. The other is the category of algebras (§6), which explains what a monad is for: a monad is a description of a kind of algebraic structure, and its algebras are the things that have that structure. The algebras of the list monad are the monoids — so the course ends by recovering the definition it began with.

§2 — The definition

An endofunctor that can flatten itself

Definition · Monad

A monad on 𝒞 is an endofunctor T : 𝒞 → 𝒞 with natural transformations η : Id ⇒ T (the unit) and μ : T2 ⇒ T (the multiplication) such that

  • associativity: μ ∘ μT = μ ∘ Tμ — the two ways to flatten T3 to T agree;
  • unit laws: μ ∘ ηT = id = μ ∘ Tη — wrapping then flattening changes nothing.

Here T2 means T ∘ T, and the symbols μT, Tμ, ηT, Tη are the whiskerings of Lesson 3. In components, at an object X:

transformationcomponent at Xin words
μTμT X : T T T X → T T Xmultiply the outer two layers
TμT(μX) : T T T X → T T Xmultiply the inner two layers
ηTηT X : T X → T T Xadd a layer outside
TηT(ηX) : T X → T T Xadd a layer inside

Take T = List, with ηX(x) = [x] and μX the function that concatenates a list of lists. On the three-layer value [[[1], [2, 3]], [[4]]] the two sides of the associativity law are:

first stepthen μ
outer layers first, μT X[[1], [2, 3], [4]][1, 2, 3, 4]
inner layers first, T(μX)[[1, 2, 3], [4]][1, 2, 3, 4]

And the unit laws on [1, 2]: adding a layer outside gives [[1, 2]], adding one inside gives [[1], [2]], and both flatten back to [1, 2].

The shape of the definition should look familiar. A monoid is a set M with a multiplication M × M → M and a unit, an element picked out by a function 1 → M, satisfying associativity and two unit laws. A monad is an endofunctor T with a multiplication T ∘ T ⇒ T and a unit Id ⇒ T, satisfying associativity and two unit laws. The only difference is that sets and their product have been replaced by endofunctors and their composition. That is the content of a sentence often quoted to alarm beginners — a monad is a monoid in the category of endofunctors — which is simply an accurate summary of the definition above.

§3 — Every adjunction gives a monad

The unit comes along; the counit gets whiskered

Take any adjunction F ⊣ U and compose: T = U ∘ F : 𝒞 → 𝒞. The adjunction hands T two natural transformations. The unit η : Id ⇒ T comes straight from Lesson 8. The counit lives in the wrong category — ε : F ∘ U ⇒ Id on the 𝒟 side — but whiskering it with U on the left and F on the right yields

μ = U ε F : T2 ⇒ T,     with components   μX = U(εF X) : U F U F X → U F X,

a way to collapse two applications of T into one. This is the operation H α K that Lesson 3 set aside for this purpose.

Theorem · Monads from adjunctions

If F ⊣ U with unit η and counit ε, then (U F, η, U ε F) is a monad on 𝒞.

The three laws come from the three facts we know about η and ε. The first unit law is the second triangle identity at the object F X. The second unit law is the first triangle identity with U applied to it. Associativity is the naturality square of ε at the arrow εF X, with U applied. Exercise 4 writes the three computations out; they have the flavour of Lesson 8’s Exercise 12.

For the free-monoid adjunction: T = List, η(x) = [x], and the counit εM multiplies out a list of elements of the monoid M. At M = List X, whose multiplication is concatenation, it concatenates a list of lists. So μ is flatten.

For a Galois connection f ⊣ g the theorem gives the closure operator of §1: the unit is x ≤ g(f(x)), and μ is the inequality obtained by applying g to the counit f(g(y)) ≤ y at y = f(x). The three laws need no checking, because in a poset any two parallel arrows are equal.

And for the currying adjunction (−) × S ⊣ (−)S on Set the theorem produces something that looks designed, though it was not:

T X = (X × S)S,

the set of functions that take a state s and return a value together with a new state. The unit sends x to the function s ↦ (x, s), which returns x and leaves the state alone. The multiplication runs such a function, obtains another one together with a new state, and runs that. Programmers call T the state monad. It is the monad of currying.

Different adjunctions can give the same monad, and passing from the adjunction to the monad forgets the category 𝒟 entirely. How much can be recovered is the question of §5 and §6.

§4 — A gallery of monads

A value, plus something extra

The examples are a programmer’s standard library, and a mathematician’s as well.

List: nondeterminism — a computation returning many possible answers; μ merges a batch of batches. Maybe: partiality — T X = X + 1, an answer or a failure ⊥ (Python’s None); η includes X, and μ collapses the two possible failures, inner and outer, into one. Writer: logging — T X = X × M for a monoid M; μ combines the two logs, and its associativity law holds because the monoid’s does — Lesson 1’s axioms, billed at last (Exercise 2 makes the dependence explicit).

The power set monad is the pair of natural transformations that Lesson 3 asked you to keep in mind: the singleton map ηX : X → P X and the union map μX : P P X → P X. Its laws are facts about unions that nobody doubts. A union of unions may be taken in either order; the union of the singletons of the elements of S is S; the union of the one-member family {S} is S. It is the list monad with order and repetition forgotten.

In every case T X reads as “a value of type X, plus something extra” — several values, possibly none, a log, a dependence on a state. The unit says “nothing extra,” and the multiplication says “two rounds of extra can be merged into one.” Computer scientists call the something extra an effect.

Each row also comes from an adjunction, as §3 says it may. List comes from the free monoid; Maybe from adding a base point to a set, the left adjoint of the forgetful functor from the pointed sets of Lesson 4; Writer from the free set with an action of M; State from currying. But the definition of a monad mentions no adjunction, and it is often easier to check the laws directly than to find one.

A warning to go with the gallery: a monad is a functor with a chosen unit and multiplication, not a property of the functor. The same functor may carry several monad structures or none (Exercise 3).

§5 — The Kleisli category

What the monad laws are for

Here is the question the laws answer. An effectful function from A to B is an ordinary arrow A → T B: it returns a B, plus effect. Can effectful functions be composed? Given f : A → T B and g : B → T C, the types refuse to chain directly — f lands in T B, and g wants a bare B. The monad supplies the adapter: apply T to g (Lesson 2: functors act on arrows), then flatten the doubled effect with μ:

Now the point of the whole construction. Define the Kleisli category Kl(T): same objects as 𝒞, but an arrow A ⇸ B is an arrow A → T B of 𝒞, composed as above, with ηA : A → T A as the identity. For this to be a category — Lesson 1’s two laws — you need exactly: composition associative, and η neutral on both sides. Unwind those requirements (Exercise 7) and they are, one for one, the monad laws. The associativity square is what makes chained effectful calls bracket-independent; the unit laws are what make a pure value a no-op in a pipeline.

Two categories from earlier lessons whose arrows were not functions turn out to be Kleisli categories of monads on Set.

  • Maybe. An arrow A → B + 1 is a partial function from A to B: where it returns ⊥, it is undefined. The Kleisli composite of two partial functions is defined exactly where both steps are. So Kl(Maybe) is the category Par of sets and partial functions from Lesson 4.
  • Power set. An arrow f : A → P B is a relation from A to B: a is related to the elements of f(a). The Kleisli composite sends a to the union of the sets g(b) for b in f(a) — the set of all c reachable from a through some b — which is the composite of relations. The identity η relates each element to itself alone. So Kl(P) is the category Rel of Lesson 1.

For List the Kleisli arrows are functions with several outcomes, in order and with repetition (Exercise 5); for Writer they are functions that also emit a log, and composition concatenates the logs.

Bind

Programmers usually meet the package in another form. For g : A → T B, put

g* = μB ∘ T g : T A → T B,

the extension of g to inputs that already carry an effect. Then Kleisli composition is g ∘K f = g* ∘ f, and the operation m ↦ g*(m), written m >>= g and pronounced bind, is Kleisli composition with the first argument already evaluated. Haskell’s do-notation and Rust’s and_then are this operation. JavaScript’s promise chaining is a close relative, though promises flatten themselves automatically and so do not obey the laws in every case. The unit and the extension operation determine the monad, and the three laws can be restated in terms of them (Exercise 8).

The first answer to the converse question

Does every monad come from an adjunction? The Kleisli category gives one.

Theorem · The Kleisli adjunction

For a monad T on 𝒞 there is an adjunction FT ⊣ UT between 𝒞 and Kl(T) whose monad is T. The functor FT is the identity on objects and sends f : A → B to ηB ∘ f. The functor UT sends an object A to T A and a Kleisli arrow g : A → T B to its extension g* : T A → T B.

Proof. That UT preserves identities and composition is the content of the laws for extension in Exercise 8, and FT is a functor by the naturality of η and a unit law. An arrow FT A ⇸ B of the Kleisli category is, by definition, an arrow A → T B of 𝒞; and T B is UT B. So

Kl(T)(FT A, B) = 𝒞(A, UT B),

and the bijection demanded by the definition of an adjunction is the identity function. It is natural in both variables. Finally UT FT A = T A, and on arrows UT FT f = (ηB ∘ f)* = μB ∘ T ηB ∘ T f = T f, by a unit law: the composite UT ∘ FT is T, and one checks in the same way that the unit and multiplication of this adjunction are η and μ. ∎

So the answer is yes. The functor FT deserves a second look in the two examples above: it turns a function into a partial function that happens to be defined everywhere, and into the relation that is its graph. Lesson 1 already met the second of these: it is how Set sits inside Rel.

§6 — Algebras

Where the structure lives

A monad describes a kind of structure; an algebra for it is a place where that structure is actually realized. The definition is one arrow and two laws:

Definition · T-algebra

An algebra for a monad (T, η, μ) is an object X with an arrow a : T X → X (the structure map) such that a ∘ ηX = idX and a ∘ μX = a ∘ T a. A homomorphism from an algebra (X, a) to an algebra (Y, b) is an arrow h : X → Y with h ∘ a = b ∘ T h.

Read a as an evaluator: it collapses a T-shaped bundle of X’s to a single X. The first law says a trivially-wrapped value evaluates to itself; the second says a two-layer bundle evaluates to the same answer whether you merge the layers first (μ) or evaluate the inner bundles first (T a). A homomorphism is an arrow that commutes with evaluation.

For the List monad the picture is this. Think of a list [x1, …, xn] as an unevaluated product, an expression. A structure map a assigns a value to every expression. The unit law says the one-term expression [x] has the value x. The second law says that an expression built from sub-expressions may be evaluated in stages. An evaluator with these two properties is exactly a monoid structure on X: put x · y = a([x, y]) and e = a([]), and conversely let a multiply a list out (Exercise 10). And a homomorphism of algebras is a homomorphism of monoids. This closes the loop that the course opened when Lesson 1 called monoids one-object categories:

the algebras of the list monad are the monoids.

The same reading works for every monad in the gallery.

So a monad is a theory, and its algebras are the models of the theory. T X is the set of all expressions that the theory can form from the elements of X; the unit regards an element as an expression; the multiplication substitutes expressions into an expression; and an algebra is a set in which every expression has a value.

Free algebras, and the second answer

Every monad has algebras, whatever the category: for each object X, the pair (T X, μX) is one. Its two laws are a unit law and the associativity law of the monad. It is called the free algebra on X, and the name is earned: for any algebra (Y, b) and any arrow f : X → Y, there is exactly one homomorphism (T X, μX) → (Y, b) that restricts to f along ηX, namely b ∘ T f (Exercise 13). For lists this is the statement, from the first page of Lesson 8, that a function on generators extends to exactly one homomorphism.

The algebras of T and their homomorphisms form a category, written 𝒞T and called the Eilenberg–Moore category of the monad.

Theorem · The Eilenberg–Moore adjunction

The forgetful functor UT : 𝒞T → 𝒞, which sends (X, a) to X, has a left adjoint FT, which sends X to the free algebra (T X, μX). The monad of the adjunction FT ⊣ UT is T.

Proof. The universal property of the free algebra is a bijection between homomorphisms (T X, μX) → (Y, b) and arrows X → Y, sending h to h ∘ ηX, with inverse f ↦ b ∘ T f. It is natural in both variables, so FT ⊣ UT, with unit η. The composite UT FT sends X to T X. The counit at an algebra (Y, b) is the homomorphism corresponding to idY, which is b itself; at a free algebra (T X, μX) it is μX. So the multiplication UT ε FT is μ. ∎

For the list monad, 𝒞T is the category of monoids, UT is the forgetful functor, and FT is the free monoid functor: the Eilenberg–Moore adjunction of the list monad is the adjunction that Lesson 8 started from. The monad remembered everything.

Two answers, compared

A monad thus comes from at least two adjunctions, and they sit at opposite extremes. The Kleisli category is as small as it can be: it has no objects beyond those of 𝒞, and no arrows beyond those the monad requires. The Eilenberg–Moore category is as large as it can be: it contains every algebra. They are related in a simple way. A Kleisli arrow A → T B extends to a homomorphism of free algebras (T A, μA) → (T B, μB), and every homomorphism between free algebras arises from exactly one Kleisli arrow. So

Kl(T)  is equivalent to  the full subcategory of 𝒞T on the free algebras.

Any other adjunction F ⊣ U with the same monad lies in between: there are comparison functors Kl(T) → 𝒟 → 𝒞T, compatible with all the functors in sight. The second one sends an object Y of 𝒟 to the algebra (U Y, U εY).

Sometimes the two extremes coincide. Every pointed set is a free algebra for Maybe — remove the base point, and add it back — so for that monad the Kleisli category is equivalent to the whole category of algebras. That is the equivalence Par ≃ Set∗ of Lesson 4: partial functions on one side, the Kleisli category; pointed sets on the other, the algebras.

§7 — Monads by hand

Flatten, compose and evaluate on paper

The laws of a monad and of an algebra are equations between functions, and for the monads on Set they can be tested on particular values. Five computations.

The power set monad on one value

Let X = {1, 2, 3}. The multiplication takes a set of subsets to its union: μ({{1}, {1, 2}, ∅}) = {1, 2}. For the unit laws take S = {1, 3}. Wrapping outside gives {{1, 3}}, whose union is {1, 3}. Wrapping inside — applying P(η), the direct image of the singleton map — gives {{1}, {3}}, whose union is again {1, 3}.

For associativity we need a set of sets of subsets: take the two-member family consisting of {{1}, {2}} and {{2, 3}}.

first stepthen μ
outer layers first{ {1}, {2}, {2, 3} }{1, 2, 3}
inner layers first{ {1, 2}, {2, 3} }{1, 2, 3}

Kleisli composition is composition of relations

Lesson 1’s Exercise 17 composed two relations: R from {1, 2} to {a, b, c}, with pairs (1, a), (1, b), (2, c); and S from {a, b, c} to {x, y}, with pairs (a, x), (b, y), (c, y). As Kleisli arrows for the power set monad they are the functions

f(1) = {a, b},   f(2) = {c};       g(a) = {x},   g(b) = {y},   g(c) = {y}.

The Kleisli composite runs f, applies g to each result, and takes the union:

(g ∘K f)(1) = g(a) ∪ g(b) = {x, y},       (g ∘K f)(2) = g(c) = {y}.

As a relation that is (1, x), (1, y), (2, y) — the composite S ∘ R found in Lesson 1.

Partial functions

Let f be “subtract one,” defined on the natural numbers n ≥ 1, and g be “halve,” defined on the even ones. As Kleisli arrows for Maybe they return ⊥ where they are undefined. The composite g ∘K f at 3: f(3) = 2, and g(2) = 1. At 4: f(4) = 3, and g(3) = ⊥. At 0: f(0) = ⊥, and the extension of g sends ⊥ to ⊥ without consulting g. A failure at any stage is a failure of the whole.

An algebra, and an evaluator that is not one

Let a be the sum of a list of integers. It is a structure map for the list monad: a([x]) = x, and on the two-layer value [[1, 2], [3]],

a(μ([[1, 2], [3]])) = a([1, 2, 3]) = 6,

a(T a([[1, 2], [3]])) = a([3, 3]) = 6.

The corresponding monoid is the integers under addition. Now let m be the average of a non-empty list of numbers. The unit law holds, since the average of [x] is x. But

m(μ([[1, 2], [3]])) = m([1, 2, 3]) = 2,

m(T m([[1, 2], [3]])) = m([1.5, 3]) = 2.25.

The average of the averages is not the average. So the average is not an algebra: there is no associative operation whose repeated product it is, and indeed the obvious candidate x · y = (x + y)/2 is not associative. (The average of an empty list is undefined, so the monad in question is strictly that of non-empty lists, whose algebras are semigroups — monoids without the unit. The computation is the same.)

The algebras of a closure operator

In the group ℤ/6, let c(S) be the subgroup generated by a subset S: c({2}) = {0, 2, 4} and c({2, 3}) is all of ℤ/6. This is a monad on the poset of subsets of ℤ/6. An algebra is a subset S with c(S) ⊆ S: a subset that already contains everything it generates. There are four — {0}, {0, 3}, {0, 2, 4} and ℤ/6 — the four subgroups. The algebras of “the subgroup generated by” are the subgroups.

Optional · PythonMonad laws by enumeration

The list monad’s functor, unit and multiplication have all appeared in this course before — fmap in Lesson 2, flatten in Lesson 3 — and the laws can be checked over a systematically generated cube of nested lists, with Maybe done fully exhaustively alongside:

monad_laws.py — List and Maybe, laws by enumeration
from itertools import product as cartesian

eta  = lambda x: [x]
mu   = lambda xss: [x for xs in xss for x in xs]     # Lesson 3's flatten
fmap = lambda f, xs: [f(x) for x in xs]

def lists_over(base, maxlen=2):
    out = [[]]
    for k in range(1, maxlen + 1):
        out += [list(t) for t in cartesian(base, repeat=k)]
    return out

T1 = lists_over([0, 1]); T2 = lists_over(T1); T3 = lists_over(T2)

assert all(mu(mu(x)) == mu(fmap(mu, x)) for x in T3)          # associativity
assert all(mu(eta(x)) == x == mu(fmap(eta, x)) for x in T1)   # unit laws
print(f"List : associativity on {len(T3)} T\u00b3 values, units on {len(T1)} — pass")

J     = lambda x: ('Just', x)                        # Maybe's eta
fmapM = lambda f, m: None if m is None else J(f(m[1]))
muM   = lambda mm: None if mm is None else mm[1]     # collapse one layer

M1 = [None] + [J(x) for x in (0, 1)]
M2 = [None] + [J(m) for m in M1]
M3 = [None] + [J(m) for m in M2]

assert all(muM(muM(m)) == muM(fmapM(muM, m)) for m in M3)
assert all(muM(J(m)) == m == muM(fmapM(J, m)) for m in M1)
print(f"Maybe: associativity on {len(M3)} T\u00b3 values, units on {len(M1)} — pass")
outputList : associativity on 3307 T³ values, units on 7 — pass Maybe: associativity on 5 T³ values, units on 3 — pass

For Maybe the check is exhaustive: there are only five triple-layer values over a two-point base, and all five satisfy the law. For List, 3,307 systematically generated triples of nesting all agree that outer-first and inner-first flattening coincide.

The power set monad can be checked exhaustively too, over a two-element base. A set of sets of subsets of a two-element set is one of 216 = 65,536 values.

powerset_monad.py — unions of unions
from itertools import combinations

def powerset(xs):
    xs = list(xs)
    return [frozenset(c) for r in range(len(xs) + 1)
            for c in combinations(xs, r)]

etaP  = lambda x: frozenset([x])                        # singleton
muP   = lambda ss: frozenset(x for s in ss for x in s)  # union
fmapP = lambda f, s: frozenset(f(x) for x in s)         # direct image

P1 = powerset([0, 1]); P2 = powerset(P1); P3 = powerset(P2)
assert all(muP(muP(a)) == muP(fmapP(muP, a)) for a in P3)
assert all(muP(etaP(s)) == s == muP(fmapP(etaP, s)) for s in P1)
print(f"Powerset: associativity on {len(P3)} values,",
      f"units on {len(P1)} — pass")
outputPowerset: associativity on 65536 values, units on 4 — pass

Next, the claim of §5 that Kleisli composition for this monad is composition of relations, on the two relations of Lesson 1:

kleisli_rel.py — relations are Kleisli arrows
def kleisli(g, f):
    "g after f, for the power set monad"
    return lambda a: muP(fmapP(g, f(a)))

def compose_rel(S, R):
    "S after R, for relations given as sets of pairs"
    return {(a, c) for (a, b) in R for (b2, c) in S if b == b2}

R = {(1, 'a'), (1, 'b'), (2, 'c')}
S = {('a', 'x'), ('b', 'y'), ('c', 'y')}
f = lambda a: frozenset(b for (a2, b) in R if a2 == a)
g = lambda b: frozenset(c for (b2, c) in S if b2 == b)

graph = {(a, c) for a in (1, 2) for c in kleisli(g, f)(a)}
print("Kleisli composite   :", sorted(graph))
print("relational composite:", sorted(compose_rel(S, R)))
outputKleisli composite : [(1, 'x'), (1, 'y'), (2, 'y')] relational composite: [(1, 'x'), (1, 'y'), (2, 'y')]

evaluators.py — the sum is an algebra, the average is not
from fractions import Fraction

avg = lambda xs: Fraction(sum(xs), len(xs))       # an exact average

Going deeper

Which categories are categories of algebras?

For the list monad, the Eilenberg–Moore construction gave back the category of monoids. Is that typical? Start from any adjunction F ⊣ U between 𝒞 and 𝒟, form its monad T, and compare 𝒟 with 𝒞T by the functor of §6, which sends Y to the algebra (U Y, U εY). When this comparison is an equivalence, the functor U is called monadic: the category 𝒟 can be reconstructed from the monad alone, as its category of algebras.

The forgetful functors from monoids, groups, rings and vector spaces to Set are all monadic. So is every category of structures defined by operations and equations: the monad T X is “all expressions in the elements of X, up to the equations,” exactly as for lists. This is the sense in which monads are a category-theoretic account of algebra.

Other forgetful functors are not monadic, and the failure is instructive. The underlying-set functor from preorders has the discrete order as its left adjoint (Lesson 8, Exercise 17). The composite sends a set to itself: the monad is the identity monad, whose algebras are just sets. All the order structure has been lost. The same happens for topological spaces. Order and topology are not algebraic structure on a set, in the sense of operations and equations — and the monad detects this.

There are surprises in the other direction. The category of compact Hausdorff spaces is monadic over Set: its monad sends a set to the set of ultrafilters on it, and an algebra is a rule assigning a limit point to every ultrafilter. Which functors are monadic is settled by a theorem of Beck, the monadicity theorem, which is one of the natural next things to learn.

Everything in this lesson can be dualized. An endofunctor with a counit T ⇒ Id and a comultiplication T ⇒ T2 is a comonad; every adjunction F ⊣ U gives one on 𝒟, namely F ∘ U. Where monads model computations that produce effects, comonads model computations that consume a context.

One functor, several candidate monads. Let T X = X × X on Set, with η(x) = (x, x). An element of T T X is a pair of pairs ((a, b), (c, d)). Consider three candidates for the multiplication, each described by what it makes of that element:

Show solution

(a) ηT X(a, b) = ((a, b), (a, b)): duplicate the whole pair. T ηX (a, b) = ((a, a), (b, b)): duplicate each entry.

Exercise 4arrows-only

The Lesson 8 connection, in full. Let F ⊣ U with unit η and counit ε, and set T = UF, μ = UεF (components μX = U(εF X)). Prove the three monad laws:

  1. μ ∘ ηT = id — i.e. μX ∘ ηT X = idT X;
  2. μ ∘ Tη = id — i.e. μX ∘ UF(ηX) = idT X;
  3. μ ∘ μT = μ ∘ Tμ.

Hint: (a) and (b) are the two triangle identities, one whiskered by U, the other instantiated at F X. For (c), apply U to the naturality square of ε at the arrow εF X.

Show solution

(a) The second triangle identity says U(εY) ∘ ηU Y = idU Y for every Y; instantiate Y = F X: U(εF X) ∘ ηU F X = id, which is exactly μX ∘ ηT X = idT X.

(b) The first triangle identity says εF X ∘ F(ηX) = idF X; apply the functor U to both sides (Lesson 2: equal arrows have equal images, composition splits): U(εF X) ∘ UF(ηX) = idUF X — which is μX ∘ T(ηX) = idT X.

(c) Naturality of ε at the arrow εF X : FUF X → F X reads εF X ∘ FU(εF X) = εF X ∘ εFUF X. Apply U: μX ∘ UFU(εF X) = μX ∘ U(εFUF X) — and reading the two sides as components, the left is μX ∘ T(μX) and the right is μX ∘ μT X. That is associativity. Three laws, three ingredients: one triangle each for the units, one naturality square for associativity — the same toolkit as Lesson 8’s Exercise 12, run in reverse. (The converse is also true — every monad arises from an adjunction, in two standard ways: the Kleisli construction of §5 and the Eilenberg–Moore construction of §6.)

Exercise 6by hand

The power set monad and relations (§5, §7). Let f : {1, 2, 3} → P({a, b}) be given by f(1) = {a}, f(2) = {a, b}, f(3) = ∅, and g : {a, b} → P({x, y, z}) by g(a) = {x, y}, g(b) = {y, z}.

  1. Compute the Kleisli composite g ∘K f at 1, 2 and 3.
  2. Write f, g and the composite as relations — sets of pairs — and check that the composite is the composite of relations.
  3. Which relation is the identity of the Kleisli category at {a, b}?
  4. The functor FP of §5 sends a function h to the Kleisli arrow η ∘ h. Which relations arise in this way?
Show solution

(a) (g ∘K f)(1) = g(a) = {x, y}. (g ∘K f)(2) = g(a) ∪ g(b) = {x, y, z}. (g ∘K f)(3) is the union of no sets at all: ∅.

(b) f is {(1, a), (2, a), (2, b)}; g is {(a, x), (a, y), (b, y), (b, z)}. Chaining pairs through a common middle element gives (1, x), (1, y), (2, x), (2, y), (2, z) — the pair (2, y) arises twice, through a and through b, and a set records it once. That is the relation in (a).

(c) The unit η, which sends a to {a} and b to {b}: the relation {(a, a), (b, b)}, equality.

(d) η ∘ h sends each x to the one-element set {h(x)}. As a relation it is the graph of h. The relations of this form are those in which every element is related to exactly one element — the answer to Lesson 1’s Exercise 17(c).

Exercise 7structure

The full circle. Take the Kleisli data at face value: arrows A ⇸ B are arrows A → T B, composition is g ∘K f = μ ∘ Tg ∘ f, and the proposed identity on A is ηA.

  1. Show f ∘K η = f and η ∘K f = f, identifying which monad unit law each uses (and where naturality of η sneaks in).
  2. Show that associativity of ∘K follows from the monad’s associativity law together with naturality of μ. (Expand both bracketings of h ∘K g ∘K f and slide μ’s past T’s.)
Show solution

(a) Right identity: f ∘K ηA = μ ∘ Tf ∘ ηA. Naturality of η at f : A → TB slides it forward: Tf ∘ ηA = ηT B ∘ f, so the composite is μ ∘ ηT ∘ f = f by the first unit law. Left identity: ηB ∘K f = μ ∘ TηB ∘ f = f directly by the second unit law. Each unit law serves exactly one side — that is why there are two.

(b) Expand: h ∘K (g ∘K f) = μ ∘ Th ∘ μ ∘ Tg ∘ f, while (h ∘K g) ∘K f = μ ∘ T(μ ∘ Th ∘ g) ∘ f = μ ∘ Tμ ∘ T²h ∘ Tg ∘ f. In the first expression, slide Th past μ using μ’s naturality at h: Th ∘ μ = μ ∘ T²h, giving μ ∘ μT ∘ T²h ∘ Tg ∘ f. The two expressions now differ only in their heads — μ ∘ μT versus μ ∘ Tμ — and the associativity square says those are equal. ∎

So: Kl(T) is a category precisely because T is a monad. Lesson 1 asked for two laws and the course spent nine lessons discovering how much they carry; here, at the end, the same two laws re-derive themselves as the correctness conditions for effectful programming. The snake has its tail.

The state monad (§3). Let S be a set and T X = (X × S)S, the monad of the currying adjunction (−) × S ⊣ (−)S.

  1. Use the unit of the adjunction (Lesson 8, Exercise 10) to write down ηX.

Show solution

(a) ηX(x) is the function s ↦ (x, s).

(b) μX = U(εF X), where F X = X × S, U = (−)S, and εF X is eval : (X × S)S × S → X × S. Applying U means composing with eval afterwards. So μX(k) is the function s ↦ eval(k(s)), and if k(s) = (k′, s′) then eval(k′, s′) = k′(s′).

(c) By currying, a function A → (B × S)S corresponds to a function A × S → B × S: take the input and the current state, return the output and the new state. Let f and g be Kleisli arrows, corresponding to f̄ : A × S → B × S and ḡ : B × S → C × S. Then (g ∘K f)(a) is μ applied to the function s ↦ (g(b), s′), where (b, s′) = f̄(a, s); by (b) that is the function s ↦ g(b)(s′) = ḡ(b, s′) = ḡ(f̄(a, s)). So the composite corresponds to ḡ ∘ f̄.

A function with a side effect on a state, composed with another, is just a function that passes the state along. The state monad says so in the language of this lesson, and the Kleisli category makes the state-passing invisible.

Exercise 10structure

Show that a List-algebra on a set X is the same thing as a monoid structure on X. Given a structure map a : List X → X satisfying the two algebra laws, define e = a([]) and x · y = a([x, y]), and derive:

  1. the unit laws e · x = x = x · e,
  2. associativity of · — evaluate the two-layer value [[x, y], [z]] both ways the algebra law allows.
Show solution

The key consequence of the laws (worth deriving first) is that a evaluates concatenations pairwise: applying the second law to [xs, ys] ∈ List(List X) gives a(xs ⧺ ys) = a([a(xs), a(ys)])= a(xs) · a(ys).

(a) e · x = a([a([]), a([x])]); by the pairwise rule backwards this is a([] ⧺ [x]) = a([x]), and the first algebra law (a ∘ η = id) gives a([x]) = x. The right unit law mirrors.

(b) Evaluate [[x, y], [z]]: merging layers first (μ) gives a([x, y, z]); evaluating inner bundles first (T a) gives a([x·y, z]) = (x·y)·z. The algebra law equates them, so a([x,y,z]) = (x·y)·z — and running the same computation on [[x], [y, z]] gives a([x,y,z]) = x·(y·z). Both bracketings equal the same three-element evaluation: associativity. Conversely a monoid defines a = “multiply the list out” (Lesson 8’s fold_map of the identity), and the two constructions invert each other. Monoids are the algebras of the List monad — the course’s first definition and its last, revealed as the same idea seen from opposite ends.

Exercise 12structure

Maybe, pointed sets and partial functions (§6).

  1. Let a : X + 1 → X be an algebra for the Maybe monad. Show that the unit law forces a(x) = x for x in X, and that the second law then holds whatever element a(⊥) is.
  2. Conclude that an algebra is a pointed set, and a homomorphism a function preserving the chosen points.
  3. Describe the free algebra on a set X, and show that every algebra is isomorphic to a free one.
  4. Explain how (c), with §6, gives the equivalence Par ≃ Set∗ of Lesson 4.
Show solution

(a) The unit includes X in X + 1, so a ∘ η = id says a(x) = x. For the second law, an element of (X + 1) + 1 is an element x of X, the inner ⊥, or the outer ⊥. On x, both a ∘ μ and a ∘ T a give x. On the inner ⊥: μ gives ⊥ and then a gives a(⊥); while T a gives the element a(⊥) of X, and then a fixes it. On the outer ⊥: μ gives ⊥, then a(⊥); T a leaves it as ⊥, then a(⊥). Equal in every case.

(b) So the only datum is the element x0 = a(⊥) of X, and it may be any element: an algebra is a set with a chosen point. A homomorphism h must satisfy h(a(⊥)) = b(T h(⊥)) = b(⊥), that is, h(x0) = y0; on the elements of X the condition is automatic.

(c) The free algebra on X is X + 1, with ⊥ as its chosen point. Given any pointed set (Y, y0), let X be Y with y0 removed. The function X + 1 → Y that is the identity on X and sends ⊥ to y0 is a bijection preserving the points: an isomorphism of algebras.

(d) §6 showed that the Kleisli category is equivalent to the full subcategory of free algebras. By (c) every algebra is isomorphic to a free one, so that subcategory is equivalent to the whole category of algebras (Lesson 4: a full and faithful functor that is essentially surjective is an equivalence). The Kleisli category of Maybe is Par, and its category of algebras is Set∗.

Exercise 13arrows-only

Free algebras (§6). Let (T, η, μ) be a monad on 𝒞.

  1. Show that (T X, μX) is an algebra.
  2. Let (Y, b) be an algebra and f : X → Y any arrow. Show that f̂ = b ∘ T f is a homomorphism from (T X, μX) to (Y, b), and that f̂ ∘ ηX = f.
  3. Show that f̂ is the only homomorphism with that property.
Show solution

(a) The unit law for an algebra with structure map μX reads μX ∘ ηT X = id, which is the first unit law of the monad. The second reads μX ∘ μT X = μX ∘ T μX, which is associativity.

(b) Homomorphism: f̂ ∘ μX = b ∘ T f ∘ μX = b ∘ μY ∘ T T f by the naturality of μ; this is b ∘ T b ∘ T T f by the second algebra law for b; and that is b ∘ T(b ∘ T f) = b ∘ T f̂. Restriction: f̂ ∘ ηX = b ∘ T f ∘ ηX = b ∘ ηY ∘ f by the naturality of η, and b ∘ ηY = id by the first algebra law.

(c) Let h be a homomorphism from (T X, μX) to (Y, b) with h ∘ ηX = f. By the second unit law of the monad, h = h ∘ μX ∘ T ηX. Since h is a homomorphism, h ∘ μX = b ∘ T h. So h = b ∘ T h ∘ T ηX = b ∘ T(h ∘ ηX) = b ∘ T f.

Parts (b) and (c) together are the bijection of the Eilenberg–Moore adjunction. Every law was used once: both unit laws and associativity of the monad, both laws of the algebra b, and both naturality squares.

Monads and orders

§5–§6, Going deeper · Exercises 14–15

Exercise 14structure

Monads on a poset. Let P be a poset, regarded as a category.

  1. Show that a monad on P is exactly a monotone map c : P → P with x ≤ c(x) and c(c(x)) ≤ c(x) for all x. Why is there nothing further to check?
  2. Show that c(c(x)) = c(x).
  3. What is an algebra for c?
  4. When is there a Kleisli arrow from x to y? Verify directly, from the properties in (a), that Kleisli arrows compose.
Show solution

(a) An endofunctor of P is a monotone map. A natural transformation Id ⇒ c is a family of arrows x → c(x), that is, the inequalities x ≤ c(x); naturality is automatic in a thin category. Likewise μ is the family of inequalities c(c(x)) ≤ c(x). The three monad laws are equations between parallel arrows, and in a poset parallel arrows are always equal.

(b) The unit at the element c(x) gives c(x) ≤ c(c(x)). With c(c(x)) ≤ c(x) and antisymmetry, the two are equal.

(c) An arrow c(x) → x: an element with c(x) ≤ x, hence, by the unit, with c(x) = x — a closed element. The two algebra laws are again automatic. So the Eilenberg–Moore category of a closure operator is the poset of closed elements.

(d) A Kleisli arrow x ⇸ y is an arrow x → c(y): it exists when x ≤ c(y). Identities: x ≤ c(x). Composition: if x ≤ c(y) and y ≤ c(z), then c(y) ≤ c(c(z)) = c(z) by monotonicity and (b), so x ≤ c(z). The Kleisli category is the preorder “x lies in the closure of y.”

For c = “the subgroup generated by,” the algebras are the subgroups (§7), and a Kleisli arrow from S to S′ says that everything in S can be generated from S′.

Exercise 15think

A forgetful functor that is not monadic (Going deeper). Lesson 8’s Exercise 17 showed that the underlying-set functor U from preorders to sets has a left adjoint D, the discrete order.

  1. Compute the monad T = U ∘ D, with its unit and multiplication.
  2. What is an algebra for this monad? What is the Eilenberg–Moore category?
  3. What does the comparison functor of §6 do to a preorder? Is it an equivalence?
  4. Contrast with the adjunction between sets and monoids.
Show solution

(a) D X is the set X with the discrete order, and U forgets the order again: T X = X. The unit is the identity function, and so is the multiplication. T is the identity monad.

(b) A structure map a : X → X must satisfy a ∘ η = id, that is, a = id. So every set is an algebra in exactly one way, every function is a homomorphism, and the Eilenberg–Moore category is Set.

(c) It sends a preorder P to the algebra (U P, U εP) — the underlying set, with the only structure map there is. It is the functor U itself, and it is not an equivalence: it is not full, since most functions between preorders are not monotone.

(d) For monoids the comparison functor is an equivalence: an algebra for the list monad is a monoid, and nothing is lost. The difference is that a monoid structure consists of operations on the underlying set, obeying equations, and a monad can record those; an order is a relation, and the monad of the adjunction does not see it at all.

Recap

What the course leaves you with

  1. A monad is an endofunctor T with a unit η : Id ⇒ T and a multiplication μ : T2 ⇒ T obeying associativity and two unit laws — a monoid made of functors. Every adjunction yields one: T = U F, μ = U ε F.
  2. The gallery: List, Maybe, Writer, power set, State, and on a poset the closure operators. T X is an X with something extra; η adds nothing; μ merges two rounds into one.
  3. The Kleisli category of arrows A → T B is a category precisely because the monad laws hold — Lesson 1’s two axioms, rediscovered as the specification of composable effects. Partial functions and relations are the Kleisli categories of Maybe and of the power set. bind is Kleisli composition.
  4. An algebra is an arrow a : T X → X that evaluates, compatibly with η and μ. The algebras of the list monad are the monoids; of Maybe, the pointed sets; of a closure operator, the closed elements.
  5. Every monad comes from an adjunction, in two extreme ways: the Kleisli adjunction and the Eilenberg–Moore adjunction, with the Kleisli category sitting inside the algebras as the free ones.
  6. Across the whole course: an object is known by its arrows (Yoneda); the best objects are defined by universal properties, which are representations; adjunctions organise universal properties into pairs of functors; and monads are what an adjunction leaves behind on one side.
The road from here

Ten lessons down; the subject is wide open

Natural next steps: monadicity and the general theory of algebraic structures; presheaf categories and topoi, where Yoneda becomes a way of life; Kan extensions (“all concepts are Kan extensions”); monoidal and enriched categories, where the hom-sets themselves acquire structure. For books — Leinster’s Basic Category Theory for rigor at this course’s scope, Awodey’s Category Theory and Riehl’s Category Theory in Context to go deeper, Milewski’s Category Theory for Programmers to keep the code angle, and Mac Lane’s Categories for the Working Mathematician when you are ready for the source.
