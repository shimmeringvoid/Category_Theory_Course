§1 — Why build categories?

Because the best object is always best in some category

Until now categories were found: sets, groups, orders, monoids. This lesson manufactures them. Each construction takes one or two categories and returns a new one, and the reason to care is a single observation that organises the second half of the course.

Lessons 6 to 8 are about objects that are best possible solutions to a problem — the best lower bound, the best pair, the best approximation. “Best” will always be given the same meaning: there is exactly one arrow to it from every competitor, or exactly one arrow from it to every competitor. But the competitors are not usually the objects of the category we started with. They are objects equipped with something — an object with a pair of arrows, an object with an arrow to A — and for “exactly one arrow” to make sense they must form a category of their own. The constructions of this lesson are how those categories get built. The triangle at the top of the page is one arrow in the most important of them.

Here is the whole kit. Half of it you have met in exercises; this lesson makes it systematic.

§2 — Opposites

Reverse every arrow, and get a second theorem free

Lesson 1, Exercise 4 built the opposite category 𝒞op: the same objects, and for each arrow f : X → Y of 𝒞 an arrow f : Y → X of 𝒞op, composed in the reverse order.

In familiar cases the opposite is a familiar thing:

  • The opposite of a preorder is the same set with the order reversed: (ℕ, ≤)op = (ℕ, ≥).
  • The opposite of a monoid, viewed as a one-object category, is the monoid with its multiplication reversed: a ∗ b = b · a. For a commutative monoid nothing changes.
  • Relop is isomorphic to Rel, by taking converses (Lesson 1, Exercise 19).
  • Setop is something genuinely new. An arrow X → Y in Setop is a function from Y to X, and there is no way to reinterpret it as a function from X to Y. Opposite categories need not be categories “of functions” in any sense — which is one reason the abstract definition of Lesson 1 earns its keep.

Dual notions

Every notion defined by arrows has a dual: interpret the definition in 𝒞op and see what it says about 𝒞. Reversal exchanges sources with targets, “composing before” with “composing after,” and so:

For instance: f is monic in 𝒞op means that f ∘op h = f ∘op k implies h = k; unwinding the reversed composition, that says h ∘ f = k ∘ f implies h = k in 𝒞 — which is the statement that f is epic in 𝒞.

Theorem · The duality principle

Suppose a statement about categories, phrased in terms of objects, arrows, identities and composition, is true in every category. Then its dual — the statement obtained by reversing every arrow and the order of every composite — is also true in every category.

Proof. Let S be the statement and Sop its dual, and let 𝒞 be any category. Saying that Sop holds in 𝒞 is, by construction, the same as saying that S holds in 𝒞op. But 𝒞op is a category (Lesson 1, Exercise 4), and S holds in every category. ∎

The principle halves the work of the subject. Lesson 1 proved that every section is monic, and then, separately, that every retraction is epic. The second proof was unnecessary: it is the dual of the first. From here on, proofs of dual statements will simply be omitted, with the words “dually” or “by duality.”

One caution. Duality applies to statements true in all categories. A statement about one particular category — “in Set every epimorphism has a section” — dualizes to a statement about Setop, not about Set.

§3 — Products of categories

Two categories, side by side in each coordinate

Lesson 1, Exercise 5 defined the product category 𝒞 × 𝒟. Its objects are pairs (C, D), its arrows are pairs (f, g), and everything — sources, targets, identities, composition — is done separately in each slot:

(f′, g′) ∘ (f, g) = (f′ ∘ f, g′ ∘ g).

  • The product of two preorders is their product set with the componentwise order: (x, y) ≤ (x′, y′) when x ≤ x′ and y ≤ y′.
  • The product of two monoids, as one-object categories, is their direct product.
  • 𝟚 × 𝟚 has four objects and 3 × 3 = 9 arrows, and it is the commuting square of Lesson 1, Exercise 12. The diagonal of the square is the arrow (u, u).

There are projection functors 𝒞 × 𝒟 → 𝒞 and 𝒞 × 𝒟 → 𝒟, which forget one coordinate. The main use of product categories, though, is to give an exact meaning to “a functor of two variables.”

Definition · Bifunctor

A bifunctor is a functor out of a product category, T : 𝒞 × 𝒟 → ℰ.

A bifunctor is functorial in each variable separately — fix an object in one slot and what remains is a functor in the other — and the two actions commute with each other, since in the product category

(f, g) = (f, id) ∘ (id, g) = (id, g) ∘ (f, id).

Two bifunctors matter above all others. The cartesian product of sets is a bifunctor Set × Set → Set: it sends a pair of functions (f, g) to the function f × g acting coordinatewise. And the hom-sets of any locally small category form a bifunctor, covariant in one variable and contravariant in the other:

𝒞(−, −) : 𝒞op × 𝒞 → Set,     (f, g) ↦ (h ↦ g ∘ h ∘ f).

It packages the two hom functors of Lesson 2 into one: fixing the first variable gives 𝒞(A, −), fixing the second gives 𝒞(−, A). When Lesson 3 called a family of isomorphisms “natural in both variables,” it meant: a natural isomorphism between two bifunctors.

§4 — Subcategories

Keep some of the objects, some of the arrows

Definition · Subcategory

A subcategory 𝒮 of a category 𝒞 is a selection of some objects of 𝒞 and some arrows of 𝒞 such that

  • every selected arrow has its source and target among the selected objects;
  • the identity of every selected object is selected;
  • the composite of two selected arrows is selected.

Then 𝒮 is a category in its own right, with composition inherited from 𝒞. It is a full subcategory if it contains every arrow of 𝒞 between its objects, and a wide subcategory if it contains every object of 𝒞.

A full subcategory is specified by choosing objects only; a wide one by choosing arrows only. Most subcategories in practice are one or the other.

SubcategoryofKind

The last row deserves a name: the isomorphisms of any category, together with all its objects, form a wide subcategory called its core. The three conditions hold by Lesson 1, Exercise 10 — identities are isomorphisms, and isomorphisms compose — and the core is a groupoid.

The inclusion of a subcategory is a functor, always faithful, and full exactly when the subcategory is full: the two uses of the word agree. The closure conditions are not automatic, as Lesson 1, Exercise 3 showed: the functions with a fixed point, or the functions that are not identities, do not form subcategories of Set.

Careful · The image of a functor need not be a subcategory

It is tempting to think that the objects and arrows hit by a functor F : 𝒞 → 𝒟 always form a subcategory of 𝒟. They need not. Let 𝒞 have four objects A, B, B′, C and just two non-identity arrows, f : A → B and g : B′ → C — which cannot be composed, since B ≠ B′. Let F map 𝒞 to the chain 𝟛 by sending A ↦ 0, both B and B′ to 1, and C ↦ 2. The image contains the arrows 0 → 1 and 1 → 2 but not their composite 0 → 2. Gluing two objects together created a composable pair that the source never had. (When F is injective on objects this cannot happen: Exercise 6.)

§5 — Arrow categories

Promote the arrows to objects

So far the objects of each new category were objects of an old one. The next constructions take the decisive step of treating arrows as objects.

Definition · The arrow category

The arrow category 𝒞→ has

  • as objects, the arrows f : A → B of 𝒞;
  • as arrows from f : A → B to g : C → D, the pairs (a, b) of arrows a : A → C and b : B → D of 𝒞 with g ∘ a = b ∘ f — the commuting squares with f and g as opposite sides.

Composition places two squares side by side: (a′, b′) ∘ (a, b) = (a′ ∘ a, b′ ∘ b). The identity on f is (idA, idB).

That squares compose is the pasting argument of Lesson 3: if two adjacent squares commute then so does the outer rectangle. Indeed Lesson 3 met this category already, as the functor category [𝟚, 𝒞] — a functor 𝟚 → 𝒞 is an arrow, and a natural transformation between two of them is a commuting square.

Three functors connect 𝒞→ with 𝒞: source and target, which send the object f : A → B to A and to B (and a square to its top or bottom side), and in the other direction the functor sending each object A to its identity arrow.

In a preorder, an object of the arrow category is a pair x ≤ y — an interval — and there is an arrow from the interval x ≤ y to the interval x′ ≤ y′ exactly when x ≤ x′ and y ≤ y′. So the arrow category of a preorder is again a preorder, the preorder of its intervals. For the chain 𝟚 the intervals are 0 ≤ 0, 0 ≤ 1 and 1 ≤ 1, each below the next:

𝟚→ ≅ 𝟛.

§6 — Slice categories

Everything, as seen from one object

The triangle at the top of the page is the picture for the most useful construction in the kit. Fix an object A of 𝒞, and consider not the objects of 𝒞 but the objects equipped with an arrow to A.

Definition · Slice category

The slice category 𝒞/A has

  • as objects, the arrows of 𝒞 with target A — pairs (X, f) with f : X → A;
  • as arrows from (X, f) to (Y, g), the arrows h : X → Y of 𝒞 with g ∘ h = f — the commuting triangles over A.

Composition and identities are those of 𝒞.

Composition stays inside the slice: if g ∘ h = f and k ∘ h′ = g, then k ∘ (h′ ∘ h) = (k ∘ h′) ∘ h = g ∘ h = f. The associativity and identity laws are inherited from 𝒞. An object of 𝒞/A is often called an object over A, and an arrow of the slice a map over A.

Four slices worth knowing

i. In an order, the slice is a down-set

Let P be a preorder and a one of its elements. An object of P/a is an arrow into a, that is, an element x with x ≤ a. An arrow from x to y over a is just x ≤ y, the triangle commuting automatically. So P/a is the down-set of a: everything below it, with the same order. In the divisibility order on {1, 2, 3, 6}, the slice over 2 is the chain 1 | 2, and the slice over 6 is the whole diamond.

ii. Sets over A are families of sets

An object of Set/A is a function p : E → A. Read it as a family of sets indexed by A: for each a ∈ A there is the fibre p−1(a), the set of elements lying over a, and E is the union of its fibres. If A is a set of dormitories and E a set of students, p assigns each student a dormitory and the fibres are the residents of each one.

An arrow of Set/A from p : E → A to q : E′ → A is a function h with q ∘ h = p: it must send each element to an element over the same point of A. So h is the same thing as a family of functions, one between each pair of corresponding fibres. This can be stated as an equivalence in the sense of Lesson 4. Regard the set A as a discrete category; a functor A → Set is then exactly a family of sets indexed by A, and

Set/A ≃ [A, Set].

The functor from left to right sends p to its family of fibres. It is full and faithful by what was just said, and essentially surjective because any family (Sa) is the family of fibres of the evident map from the disjoint union of the Sa to A. For a two-element set this reads Set/2 ≃ Set × Set: a set over {0, 1} is a pair of sets.

iii. The slice of a monoid

Let M be a monoid, as a category with one object ∗. An object of M/∗ is an arrow into ∗ — an element m. An arrow from m to n is an element h with n · h = m. For the monoid (ℕ, +) this says n + h = m: there is such an h exactly when n ≤ m, and then only one. So

(ℕ, +)/∗ ≅ (ℕ, ≥),

the natural numbers in descending order. A construction can change the character of a category completely: here a one-object category became a thin one.

iv. The identity is the best object over A

Every slice has one distinguished object: A itself, with its identity arrow. From any object (X, f) there is exactly one arrow to (A, idA) — an arrow h with idA ∘ h = f can only be h = f, and f qualifies. Lesson 6 will call an object with this property terminal. The identity arrow is the terminal object of the slice.

Coslices

Dually, the coslice category A/𝒞 has as objects the arrows out of A, pairs (X, f) with f : A → X, and as arrows from (X, f) to (Y, g) the arrows h : X → Y with h ∘ f = g. An object of the coslice is an object under A. Nothing new needs proving: A/𝒞 is the opposite of the slice of 𝒞op over A.

The standard example brings back a category from Lesson 4. Let 1 be a one-element set. An object of 1/Set is a function 1 → X, which is a set X together with a chosen element; an arrow is a function that carries the chosen element to the chosen element. So 1/Set is the category Set∗ of pointed sets. In a preorder, the coslice a/P is the up-set of a.

§7 — Comma categories

One construction to contain the last three

Arrow categories, slices and coslices are three instances of a single construction, and the general form is the one that the second half of the course will lean on.

Definition · Comma category

Let F : 𝒜 → 𝒞 and G : ℬ → 𝒞 be two functors into the same category. The comma category (F ↓ G) has

  • as objects, the triples (X, Y, f) where X is an object of 𝒜, Y an object of ℬ, and f : F X → G Y an arrow of 𝒞;
  • as arrows from (X, Y, f) to (X′, Y′, f′), the pairs (a, b) with a : X → X′ in 𝒜 and b : Y → Y′ in ℬ such that f′ ∘ F a = G b ∘ f.

An object is an arrow of 𝒞 that starts in the image of F and ends in the image of G; an arrow is a commuting square whose top comes from 𝒜 and whose bottom comes from ℬ. The earlier constructions are recovered by special choices, writing A also for the functor 𝟙 → 𝒞 that picks out the object A:

The comma category that explains “free”

The case to remember is neither of these. Let U : 𝒟 → 𝒞 be a functor — think of a forgetful functor — and X an object of 𝒞. The comma category (X ↓ U) has as objects the pairs (Y, f) with f : X → U Y: an object of 𝒟, together with a way of mapping X into its underlying object. An arrow from (Y, f) to (Y′, f′) is an arrow k : Y → Y′ of 𝒟 with U k ∘ f = f′.

Take U to be the forgetful functor Mon → Set and X a set. An object of (X ↓ U) is a monoid M with a function from X into its elements — a monoid in which each element of X has been given a meaning. An arrow is a homomorphism that respects those meanings. Among all these objects one is special: the monoid of lists over X, with x interpreted as the one-element list [x]. From it there is exactly one arrow to every other object. That is the precise sense in which lists are the free monoid on X, and Lesson 8 builds the theory of adjunctions on this one observation.

A comma category you can compute

Let i : ℤ → ℝ be the inclusion of the integers into the reals, both as orders, and let r be a real number. The comma category (i ↓ r) has as objects the integers n with n ≤ r, ordered as usual. It has a greatest element: the floor of r. The other comma category, (r ↓ i), consists of the integers n with r ≤ n, and has a least element: the ceiling of r. “Round down” and “round up” are the best objects of two comma categories — the pattern of §1, and a preview of Lesson 8, in the simplest possible setting.

§8 — Constructions by hand

Small categories, rebuilt on paper

For a finite category each construction is a finite procedure. Four short computations, each with a surprise in it.

An opposite and a product

The category 𝟚 has objects 0, 1 and one non-identity arrow u : 0 → 1. Its opposite has the arrow pointing the other way, u : 1 → 0. Renaming the objects — call 1 “0” and 0 “1” — turns it back into 𝟚. So 𝟚op ≅ 𝟚, though the two are not equal.

For the product 𝟚 × 𝟛, count before drawing. The arrows of a product are pairs of arrows, so it has 3 × 6 = 18 arrows, of which 2 × 3 = 6 are identities. It is the order on pairs (i, j) with i ∈ {0, 1} and j ∈ {0, 1, 2}: a ladder with two rails and three rungs.

An arrow category, counted

The arrow category of the chain 𝟛 is the preorder of its intervals (§5). The intervals are 00, 01, 02, 11, 12, 22, writing ij for i ≤ j, and ij ≤ i′j′ when i ≤ i′ and j ≤ j′. Count the arrows by counting, for each interval, the intervals above it (including itself):

interval000102111222
intervals above it653321

For instance above 01 lie 01, 02, 11, 12, 22 but not 00. In total 𝟛→ has 6 objects and 6 + 5 + 3 + 3 + 2 + 1 = 20 arrows.

Two slices of the divisibility diamond

In the order {1, 2, 3, 6} under divisibility, the slice over 2 consists of the divisors of 2: the two-element chain 1 | 2, with three arrows. The slice over 6 consists of all four elements — every one of them divides 6 — with all nine arrows. Slicing over the top element changes nothing: for a preorder with a greatest element ⊤, the slice over ⊤ is the whole preorder.

The slice of a group

Now a category that is not thin. Take the group ℤ/3 as a one-object category and form its slice over the one object. By §6(iii) the objects are the elements 0, 1, 2, and an arrow from m to n is an element h with n + h = m. In a group that equation has exactly one solution, h = m − n. So between any two objects of the slice there is exactly one arrow:

arrow m → nn = 0n = 1n = 2
m = 0021
m = 1102
m = 2210

Nine arrows, each the unique one between its endpoints. Every arrow is therefore an isomorphism, all three objects are isomorphic, and by Lesson 4 the slice is equivalent to 𝟙. The group has vanished: slicing a group over its only object produces, up to equivalence, nothing at all. (The explanation is example (iv) of §6: every object of a slice has exactly one arrow to the identity, and here every arrow is invertible.)

Optional · PythonThe constructions as functions

Each construction of this lesson is a function from finite categories to finite categories, and Lesson 1’s checker can certify every output. The setup block carries over the usual classes.

setup.py — carried over from Lessons 1 and 4
from itertools import product

class FiniteCategory:
    """objects, arrows (name -> (dom, cod)), a composition table,
       and a chosen identity arrow for each object."""
    def __init__(self, objects, arrows, comp, ident):
        self.obj, self.ar = set(objects), arrows
        self.comp, self.id = comp, ident

    def dom(self, a): return self.ar[a][0]
    def cod(self, a): return self.ar[a][1]
    def composable(self, g, f): return self.dom(g) == self.cod(f)

    def check(self):
        # 1. each identity is typed X -> X
        for X in self.obj:
            assert self.ar[self.id[X]] == (X, X)
        # 2. every composable pair has a composite, correctly typed
        for g, f in product(self.ar, repeat=2):
            if self.composable(g, f):
                gf = self.comp[(g, f)]
                assert self.ar[gf] == (self.dom(f), self.cod(g))
        # 3. identity law on both sides
        for f in self.ar:
            X, Y = self.ar[f]
            assert self.comp[(self.id[Y], f)] == f
            assert self.comp[(f, self.id[X])] == f
        # 4. associativity
        for h, g, f in product(self.ar, repeat=3):
            if self.composable(h, g) and self.composable(g, f):
                assert self.comp[(h, self.comp[(g, f)])] == \
                       self.comp[(self.comp[(h, g)], f)]
        return True

def arrows_between(C, X, Y):
    "all arrows X -> Y of the finite category C"
    return [a for a, (d, c) in C.ar.items() if d == X and c == Y]

def make_poset(objs, leq):
    "a finite preorder as a thin category; the arrow a -> b is named 'a<=b'"
    ar = {f"{a}<={b}": (a, b) for a in objs for b in objs if leq(a, b)}
    comp = {(g, f): f"{ar[f][0]}<={ar[g][1]}"
            for g in ar for f in ar if ar[f][1] == ar[g][0]}
    return FiniteCategory(objs, ar, comp, {a: f"{a}<={a}" for a in objs})

# the category 2 = ( 0 --u--> 1 )
two_ar   = {'i0':(0,0), 'i1':(1,1), 'u':(0,1)}
two_comp = {('i0','i0'):'i0', ('i1','i1'):'i1',
            ('u','i0'):'u',  ('i1','u'):'u'}
Two = FiniteCategory({0,1}, two_ar, two_comp, {0:'i0', 1:'i1'})

# (Z/3, +) as a one-object category
Z3 = FiniteCategory({'*'}, {n: ('*', '*') for n in range(3)},
                    {(a, b): (a + b) % 3
                     for a in range(3) for b in range(3)}, {'*': 0})
constructions.py — opposite, product, arrow category, slice
def opposite(C):
    ar = {a: (c, d) for a, (d, c) in C.ar.items()}
    comp = {(f, g): gf for (g, f), gf in C.comp.items()}
    return FiniteCategory(C.obj, ar, comp, C.id)

def product_category(C, D):
    objs = {(X, Y) for X in C.obj for Y in D.obj}
    ar = {(f, g): ((C.dom(f), D.dom(g)), (C.cod(f), D.cod(g)))
          for f in C.ar for g in D.ar}
    comp = {(q, p): (C.comp[(q[0], p[0])], D.comp[(q[1], p[1])])
            for q in ar for p in ar if ar[p][1] == ar[q][0]}
    return FiniteCategory(objs, ar, comp,
                          {(X, Y): (C.id[X], D.id[Y]) for X, Y in objs})

def arrow_category(C):
    "objects: arrows of C;  arrows f -> g: squares (a, b) with g.a == b.f"
    ar = {(f, a, b, g): (f, g)
          for f in C.ar for g in C.ar
          for a in arrows_between(C, C.dom(f), C.dom(g))
          for b in arrows_between(C, C.cod(f), C.cod(g))
          if C.comp[(g, a)] == C.comp[(b, f)]}
    comp = {(q, p): (p[0], C.comp[(q[1], p[1])],
                     C.comp[(q[2], p[2])], q[3])
            for q in ar for p in ar if p[3] == q[0]}
    ident = {f: (f, C.id[C.dom(f)], C.id[C.cod(f)], f) for f in C.ar}
    return FiniteCategory(set(C.ar), ar, comp, ident)

def slice_category(C, A):
    "objects: arrows into A;  arrows f -> g: h with g.h == f"
    objs = [f for f in C.ar if C.cod(f) == A]
    ar = {(f, h, g): (f, g)
          for f in objs for g in objs
          for h in arrows_between(C, C.dom(f), C.dom(g))
          if C.comp[(g, h)] == f}
    comp = {(q, p): (p[0], C.comp[(q[1], p[1])], q[2])
            for q in ar for p in ar if p[2] == q[0]}
    return FiniteCategory(set(objs), ar, comp,
                          {f: (f, C.id[C.dom(f)], f) for f in objs})

def size(C):
    return f"{len(C.obj)} objects, {len(C.ar)} arrows"

Three = make_poset({0, 1, 2}, lambda a, b: a <= b)
Div   = make_poset({1, 2, 3, 6}, lambda a, b: b % a == 0)
tests = [("2 op", opposite(Two)),
         ("2 x 2", product_category(Two, Two)),
         ("2 x 3", product_category(Two, Three)),
         ("arrows of 2", arrow_category(Two)),
         ("arrows of 3", arrow_category(Three)),
         ("Div / 2", slice_category(Div, 2)),
         ("Div / 6", slice_category(Div, 6)),
         ("Z3 / *", slice_category(Z3, '*'))]
for name, K in tests:
    assert K.check()
    print(f"{name:12}", size(K))

Going deeper

The algebra of constructions

The constructions of this lesson can be applied to their own outputs, and they obey laws of their own — an algebra one level up. A few are immediate from the definitions:

(𝒞op)op = 𝒞,     (𝒞 × 𝒟)op = 𝒞op × 𝒟op,     A/𝒞 = (𝒞op/A)op.

Others are equivalences rather than equalities, and say something worth knowing. The functor category out of a product can be taken one variable at a time,

[𝒞 × 𝒟, ℰ] ≅ [𝒞, [𝒟, ℰ]],

which is the reason a bifunctor can be regarded as a functor-valued functor — the same idea as a function of two variables being a function-valued function, which Lesson 8 studies under the name currying.

The most striking law concerns slices. A slice category is a category, so it has slices of its own. Take an object (X, f) of 𝒞/A and slice again. An object of the double slice is an arrow of 𝒞/A into (X, f): an arrow h : Y → X of 𝒞, regarded as an arrow from (Y, f ∘ h) — and the arrow Y → A carries no information, since it must be f ∘ h. So an object of the double slice is just an arrow into X, and the same collapse happens for arrows (Exercise 13):

(𝒞/A)/(X, f) ≅ 𝒞/X.

Slicing twice is slicing once. In the dormitory picture of §6: a family of sets indexed by the students, who are themselves indexed by dormitories, is simply a family indexed by the students. Facts of this kind — that a construction, iterated, stays within the kit — are what make the kit usable. Lesson 7 adds one more construction to it, the category of cones over a diagram, and with that the kit is complete enough to define every limit there is.

§9 — Exercises

Fourteen problems on building categories

Most of these ask you to carry out a construction on a small category and recognise the result. That is the right way to learn the kit: each construction has a personality, and it shows only when you watch it act. The problems are grouped by topic, following the lesson’s sections, and within each group they run from easier to harder. Exercise 14, the last, is an optional machine check, in Python, of two of the others; everything else is pencil and paper.

Opposites

§2 · Exercises 1–2

Exercise 1warm-up

Describe the opposite of each category in familiar terms.

  1. The divisibility order on the positive integers.
  2. The monoid of strings over the alphabet {a, b} under concatenation, as a one-object category.
  3. The discrete category on a set S.
Show solution

(a) The same integers with the order reversed: there is an arrow m → n exactly when n divides m — the order “is a multiple of.”

(b) The same strings with concatenation in the other order: the composite of s and t is now t s instead of s t. This monoid is not commutative, so the opposite really is a different multiplication on the same set — although reversing each string, s ↦ srev, is an isomorphism from the monoid to its opposite, because (s t)rev = trev srev.

(c) Itself. A discrete category has only identity arrows, and reversing an identity changes nothing: 𝒞op = 𝒞.

Exercise 2arrows-only

Practise the duality principle.

  1. Show directly from the definitions that an arrow is a section in 𝒞op exactly when it is a retraction in 𝒞.
  2. Lesson 1, Exercise 16 proved: if g ∘ f is monic then f is monic. Write down the dual statement, being careful about which arrow it concerns, and explain why no new proof is needed.
Show solution

(a) s is a section in 𝒞op when r ∘op s = id for some r. By definition of the opposite composition this says s ∘ r = id in 𝒞 — that s has a right inverse, i.e. is a retraction of r.

(b) Reverse every arrow and the order of every composite. “g ∘ f” becomes “f ∘ g” and “monic” becomes “epic”: if f ∘ g is epic then f is epic. Renaming the arrows to the usual convention: if g ∘ f is epic then g is epic — the arrow applied last, where the original statement concerned the arrow applied first. No proof is needed because the original statement holds in every category, in particular in 𝒞op, where it says exactly this.

Products

§3 · Exercises 3–4

Exercise 3by hand

Work out the product category 𝟚 × 𝟚 explicitly.

  1. List its four objects and nine arrows, giving the source and target of each.
  2. Match them with the objects A, B, C, D and arrows f, p, q, g, d of the commuting square of Lesson 1, Exercise 12.
  3. Without listing: how many objects and arrows does 𝟛 × 𝟛 have?
Show solution

(a) Objects: (0,0), (1,0), (0,1), (1,1). Arrows are pairs of arrows of 𝟚, and 𝟚 has the arrows i0, i1, u. The four identities are (i0, i0), (i1, i0), (i0, i1), (i1, i1). The others: (u, i0) : (0,0) → (1,0); (i0, u) : (0,0) → (0,1); (i1, u) : (1,0) → (1,1); (u, i1) : (0,1) → (1,1); and (u, u) : (0,0) → (1,1).

(b) Take A = (0,0), B = (1,0), C = (0,1), D = (1,1). Then f = (u, i0), p = (i0, u), q = (i1, u), g = (u, i1), and the diagonal d = (u, u). The equation q ∘ f = d = g ∘ p is componentwise composition: (i1, u) ∘ (u, i0) = (i1 ∘ u, u ∘ i0) = (u, u), and likewise the other way round.

(c) 3 × 3 = 9 objects, and 6 × 6 = 36 arrows, since 𝟛 has six arrows.

Exercise 4structure

Verify two laws from the Going-deeper section.

  1. Show that (𝒞 × 𝒟)op and 𝒞op × 𝒟op have the same objects, the same arrows, and the same composition.
  2. Let P and Q be preorders. Show that the product category P × Q is the preorder on pairs with (x, y) ≤ (x′, y′) exactly when x ≤ x′ and y ≤ y′.
Show solution

(a) Objects: pairs (C, D) in both. An arrow (C, D) → (C′, D′) of (𝒞 × 𝒟)op is an arrow (C′, D′) → (C, D) of 𝒞 × 𝒟, that is, a pair (f, g) with f : C′ → C and g : D′ → D. An arrow (C, D) → (C′, D′) of 𝒞op × 𝒟op is a pair consisting of an arrow C → C′ of 𝒞op and an arrow D → D′ of 𝒟op — again a pair (f, g) with f : C′ → C and g : D′ → D. Composition in both is “reverse the order, slot by slot.”

(b) An arrow (x, y) → (x′, y′) in P × Q is a pair of arrows x → x′ and y → y′. Each exists exactly when the corresponding inequality holds, and is then unique. So there is at most one arrow between two pairs, and there is one exactly when both inequalities hold.

Subcategories

§4 · Exercises 5–6

Exercise 5examples

Classify each as a full subcategory, a wide subcategory, both, neither — or not a subcategory at all.

  1. Inside Grp: the abelian groups, with all homomorphisms between them.
  2. Inside Set: all sets, with the surjective functions.
  3. Inside Set: the finite sets, with the injective functions.
  4. Inside Mat: all objects, with the matrices whose entries are all non-zero.
  5. Inside a preorder P: an arbitrary subset, with all the inequalities between its elements.
Show solution

(a) Full, not wide. (b) Wide, not full: identities are surjective and surjections compose. (c) Neither: not all objects, and not all arrows between the ones chosen — but still a subcategory.

(d) Not a subcategory. The identity matrices have zero entries off the diagonal, so identities are missing. (Composition fails too: the product of two matrices without zero entries can have a zero entry.)

(e) Full. Any subset of a preorder is a full subcategory. Nothing needs checking, because a full subcategory is determined by its objects.

Exercise 6think

Return to the warning in §4.

  1. Verify that the assignment described there is a functor from 𝒞 to 𝟛.
  2. Prove that if a functor F : 𝒞 → 𝒟 is injective on objects, then the objects and arrows in its image do form a subcategory of 𝒟.
Show solution

(a) The category 𝒞 has no composable pairs of non-identity arrows, so the only composites to check involve an identity, and those are preserved because identities go to identities. Typing is correct: f : A → B goes to an arrow 0 → 1, and g : B′ → C to an arrow 1 → 2. So F is a functor.

(b) Identities: the identity of F X is F(idX), in the image. Composition: let F f and F g be composable in 𝒟, where f : X → Y and g : Y′ → Z in 𝒞. Composable means the target of F f equals the source of F g: F Y = F Y′. Since F is injective on objects, Y = Y′. So f and g were already composable in 𝒞, and F g ∘ F f = F(g ∘ f) is in the image.

The warning’s example fails for exactly the reason this proof needs injectivity: B and B′ were sent to the same object.

Arrow categories and slices

§5–§6 · Exercises 7–11

Exercise 7by hand

Arrow categories of chains.

  1. List the objects of 𝟚→ and all arrows between them, and confirm that 𝟚→ ≅ 𝟛.
  2. §8 found that 𝟛→ has 20 arrows. Which interval is the least element of this preorder, and which the greatest? Is 𝟛→ a chain?
Show solution

(a) The objects are the three arrows of 𝟚: the intervals 00 (the identity on 0), 01 (the arrow u), and 11 (the identity on 1). Then 00 ≤ 01 since 0 ≤ 0 and 0 ≤ 1; 01 ≤ 11 since 0 ≤ 1 and 1 ≤ 1; and 00 ≤ 11. With the three identities that makes six arrows forming a chain 00 ≤ 01 ≤ 11 — the category 𝟛.

(b) Least: 00, below every interval. Greatest: 22. But 𝟛→ is not a chain: the intervals 02 and 11 are incomparable, since 0 ≤ 1 but 2 ≤ 1 fails, and 1 ≤ 0 fails the other way.

Exercise 8by hand

Compute four slices.

  1. The slice of the divisibility order on {1, 2, 3, 6} over the object 3.
  2. The slice of the chain 𝟛 over its middle object 1.
  3. The slice of the monoid (ℕ, ·) of positive integers under multiplication over its one object. Which familiar order is it?
  4. The slice of the group ℤ/2 over its one object: list the objects and all arrows.
Show solution

(a) The divisors of 3: the two-element chain 1 | 3.

(b) The elements below 1: the chain 0 ≤ 1, a copy of 𝟚.

(c) Objects are the positive integers. An arrow m → n is an h with n · h = m, which exists exactly when n divides m, and is then unique. So the slice is the positive integers ordered by “is a multiple of” — the opposite of the divisibility order. Compare §6(iii), where addition produced the opposite of the usual order.

(d) Objects: 0 and 1. An arrow m → n is the unique h with n + h = m: so there are four arrows, 0 → 0 and 1 → 1 (both given by h = 0, the identities) and 0 → 1 and 1 → 0 (both given by h = 1). The two non-identity arrows are inverse to each other. This is the category ℐ of Lesson 4 — two objects and an isomorphism — and it is equivalent to 𝟙.

Exercise 9structure

Families of sets as a slice (§6, example ii). Let A = {0, 1}. Let p : {a, b, c} → A send a, b ↦ 0 and c ↦ 1, and let q : {x, y, z, w} → A send x ↦ 0 and y, z, w ↦ 1.

  1. Write each of p and q as a pair of sets.
  2. How many arrows are there from p to q in Set/A? Compare with the number of all functions {a, b, c} → {x, y, z, w}.
  3. How many arrows from q to p?
Show solution

(a) p is the family with fibres {a, b} over 0 and {c} over 1. q has fibres {x} over 0 and {y, z, w} over 1.

(b) An arrow over A maps each fibre of p to the corresponding fibre of q: a function {a, b} → {x}, of which there is 1, and a function {c} → {y, z, w}, of which there are 3. Total 1 × 3 = 3, out of 43 = 64 functions in all.

(c) A function {x} → {a, b}: 2 choices. A function {y, z, w} → {c}: 1. Total 2.

The slice is far smaller than Set between the same sets, because its arrows must respect the indexing. That is the equivalence Set/A ≃ Set × Set at work: the count in (b) is the product of the counts in the two coordinates.

Exercise 10arrows-only

§6 showed that in any slice 𝒞/A there is exactly one arrow from each object to (A, idA).

  1. State and prove the dual fact about the coslice A/𝒞.
  2. In 1/Set, the category of pointed sets, which pointed set is this distinguished object, and what is the unique arrow from it to a pointed set (X, x0)?
Show solution

(a) In A/𝒞 there is exactly one arrow from (A, idA) to each object (X, f). An arrow (A, idA) → (X, f) is an h : A → X with h ∘ idA = f, which forces h = f; and h = f does satisfy the condition. (Or: apply §6 to 𝒞op and reverse.)

(b) The object is (1, id1): the one-element set with its only point chosen. The unique base-point-preserving function from it to (X, x0) sends the point to x0. In Lesson 6’s language, the one-point set is the initial pointed set.

Exercise 11arrows-only

Isomorphisms in a slice. Let h : (X, f) → (Y, g) be an arrow of 𝒞/A. Show that h is an isomorphism in 𝒞/A if and only if h is an isomorphism in 𝒞.

Show solution

If h has an inverse in the slice, that inverse is in particular an arrow k of 𝒞 with k ∘ h = id and h ∘ k = id, so h is an isomorphism in 𝒞.

Conversely, let h be an isomorphism in 𝒞 with g ∘ h = f. The only question is whether h−1 : Y → X is an arrow of the slice, from (Y, g) to (X, f) — that is, whether f ∘ h−1 = g. It is: f ∘ h−1 = (g ∘ h) ∘ h−1 = g. Then h−1 is an inverse of h in the slice, since composition there is composition in 𝒞.

In the language of Lesson 2: the forgetful functor 𝒞/A → 𝒞, which sends (X, f) to X, reflects isomorphisms — although it is usually not full.

Comma categories, and slices of slices

§7, Going deeper · Exercises 12–13

Exercise 12think

Let i : ℤ → ℝ be the inclusion, as in §7, and r = 2.5.

  1. List a few objects of (i ↓ r) and of (r ↓ i), and identify the greatest element of the first and the least element of the second.
  2. For which real numbers r do the two comma categories have an object in common?
  3. Replace i by the inclusion of the even integers into ℝ. What are the best objects of the two comma categories at r = 2.5 now?
Show solution

(a) (i ↓ r): the integers n ≤ 2.5, namely …, 0, 1, 2; greatest element 2, the floor. (r ↓ i): the integers n ≥ 2.5, namely 3, 4, 5, …; least element 3, the ceiling.

(b) An integer n lies in both when n ≤ r and r ≤ n, that is, when r = n. So exactly when r is itself an integer — and then floor and ceiling coincide.

(c) The greatest even integer below 2.5 is 2; the least even integer above it is 4. The construction does not care which functor is used: any monotone map into the reals has its own “floor” and “ceiling,” whenever the comma categories have greatest and least elements. Lesson 8 calls these the right and left adjoints of the map.

Exercise 13structure

Slices of slices (Going deeper). Let f : X → A be an arrow of 𝒞, regarded as the object (X, f) of 𝒞/A.

  1. Show that an arrow of 𝒞/A into (X, f) is determined by an arrow h : Y → X of 𝒞 alone.
  2. Show that an arrow between two such objects, in the double slice (𝒞/A)/(X, f), is exactly an arrow k of 𝒞 with h′ ∘ k = h — and conclude that the double slice is isomorphic to 𝒞/X.
Show solution

(a) An arrow of 𝒞/A into (X, f) is an arrow h : Y → X of 𝒞 from some object (Y, g), satisfying f ∘ h = g. So g is not extra data: it is forced to be f ∘ h. An object of the double slice is therefore just an arrow h : Y → X — an object of 𝒞/X.

(b) Let h : Y → X and h′ : Y′ → X be two such objects. An arrow between them in the double slice is an arrow k of 𝒞/A, from (Y, f ∘ h) to (Y′, f ∘ h′), with h′ ∘ k = h. Being an arrow of 𝒞/A requires (f ∘ h′) ∘ k = f ∘ h — but that follows from h′ ∘ k = h by composing with f. So the only real condition is h′ ∘ k = h, which is the condition for k to be an arrow of 𝒞/X. Objects match, arrows match, and composition is that of 𝒞 in both: the two categories are isomorphic.

Recap

What to carry forward

  1. Categories can be built: the opposite 𝒞op, the product 𝒞 × 𝒟, sub-, functor and arrow categories, slices 𝒞/A, coslices A/𝒞, and comma categories (F ↓ G), which contain the last three as special cases.
  2. The duality principle: a statement true in all categories remains true with every arrow reversed. Mono and epi, section and retraction, slice and coslice are dual pairs; one proof serves both.
  3. A bifunctor is a functor on a product category. The hom-sets form one, 𝒞op × 𝒞 → Set.
  4. Subcategories may be full (choose objects) or wide (choose arrows). The image of a functor need not be a subcategory.
  5. In a slice, the objects are arrows into A and the arrows are commuting triangles: down-sets in an order, indexed families in Set. The identity idA receives exactly one arrow from every object of 𝒞/A — the first example of a universal property.
