Natural Transformations

A way to slide one functor onto another, one arrow per object, so coherently that it commutes with everything. This is the idea the whole field was invented to make precise.

§1 — One more floor up

The maps between functors

Lesson 1 gave us categories. Lesson 2 gave us functors — the maps between categories — and noticed they bundle into a category, Cat. The reflex of the subject now fires one more time. Fix two categories 𝒞 and 𝒟; there are many functors between them. What is the right notion of a map between two functors?

This is not an afterthought — it is the reason category theory exists. Eilenberg and Mac Lane kept saying things like “there is a natural map from a space to its double dual,” and wanted to pin down what “natural” meant, as opposed to a map that merely happens to exist once you make arbitrary choices. The slogan they left behind: categories were defined in order to define functors, and functors were defined in order to define natural transformations.

The friendliest first example is an operation on lists. Reversing a list works the same way whatever its entries are, and it never looks at the entries themselves — only at the positions holding them. That uniformity, made precise, is exactly naturality. By the end of this lesson you will be able to check it by hand on any example and, if you like, by machine.

§2 — The definition

A coherent family of arrows, one per object

Suppose F and G are both functors 𝒞 → 𝒟 — same source, same target. Think of them as two ways of drawing a picture of 𝒞 inside 𝒟. A natural transformation is a way to slide the first picture onto the second.

Definition · Natural transformation

A natural transformation α : F ⇒ G assigns:

  • to each object X of 𝒞, an arrow αX : F X → G X in 𝒟 — its component at X

such that for every arrow f : X → Y in 𝒞, the naturality square commutes:

NaturalityG f ∘ αX = αY ∘ F f

Read the square at the top of the page: the two paths from F X to G Y — go across by αX then down by G f, or down by F f then across by αY — must agree. The single equation has to hold for every arrow of 𝒞 at once. That is what stops α from being an arbitrary pile of arrows: each component is pinned to its neighbours by the action of every morphism. There is no freedom to wire things up inconsistently.

§3 — Naturality in the wild

Polymorphism first: lists that never look at their entries

The cleanest examples are functions you already trust. Take F = G = List and let the component be reverse. (Recall the functor List of Lesson 2: List X is the set of finite lists of elements of X, and List f — written map f in code — applies f to every entry.) The naturality square says: for any f, mapping f after reversing equals reversing after mapping f. It holds for a structural reason — reverse only rearranges positions, it never inspects the elements, so it cannot matter whether you transform the elements before or after.

This is not a coincidence of reverse. A function whose type is genuinely polymorphic — defined uniformly for all types, blind to the contents — is forced to be natural; the naturality square comes packaged with the type signature. (This is the heart of what’s called a “free theorem.”) The same goes for safe_head : List ⇒ Maybe, concat, length seen as List ⇒ Const ℕ, and so on.

Aside · What “natural” was invented to mean

For every vector space V there is a map to its double dual V → V** sending a vector to “evaluation at that vector.” It needs no choice of basis, and it assembles into a natural transformation from the identity functor to the double-dual functor. The single dual V → V* is different: an isomorphism exists for finite-dimensional V, but only after choosing a basis, and it is not natural. The gap between “isomorphic” and “naturally isomorphic” is precisely the gap these definitions were built to capture. In mainstream mathematics the determinant is another: det is a natural transformation from GLn to the units functor, commuting with every homomorphism of commutative rings.

When every component αX is an isomorphism, α is a natural isomorphism, written F ≅ G. This is the correct sense in which two functors can be “the same.”

Lemma · Inverses are automatically natural

If α : F ⇒ G has every component invertible, the inverse components (αX)−1 assemble into a natural transformation G ⇒ F. So “natural iso” needs only that each αX be an iso — coherence of the inverse comes for free.

Proof (arrows only). Start from the naturality square G f ∘ αX = αY ∘ F f. Pre-compose with (αX)−1 and post-compose with (αY)−1; the components cancel by the inverse laws and you are left with (αY)−1 ∘ G f = F f ∘ (αX)−1, which is exactly the naturality square for the inverse family. ∎

§4 — Naturality in mathematics

Five families of arrows, and whether each is natural

Lists are a friendly first example, but naturality was invented for mathematics, and every corner of mathematics is full of it. In each example below, the pattern is the same: a family of arrows, one for each object, built by the same recipe every time. Naturality is the precise statement that the recipe does not depend on any feature of the particular object.

Singletons and unions

Let P be the covariant powerset functor of Lesson 2: P X is the set of subsets of X, and P f sends a subset S to its direct image f(S). For every set X there is a function

ηX : X → P X,     x ↦ {x}.

Is the family η a natural transformation from the identity functor to P? The square to check, for a function f : X → Y, compares two routes from X to P Y:

Across then down sends x to {x} and then to the direct image f({x}), which is {f(x)}. Down then across sends x to f(x) and then to {f(x)}. Equal: η : Id ⇒ P is natural. In the same way the union map μX : P(P X) → P X, which takes a set of subsets to their union, is natural from P ∘ P to P, because the direct image of a union is the union of the direct images. Keep this pair in mind — a transformation Id ⇒ P and a transformation P ∘ P ⇒ P — for Lesson 10, where exactly this shape is called a monad.

Diagonals and projections

Let S be the “squaring” functor on Set: S X = X × X and S f = f × f. The diagonal maps δX : X → X × X, x ↦ (x, x), form a natural transformation Id ⇒ S: both routes around the square send x to (f(x), f(x)). In the other direction, the two projections X × X → X are each natural transformations S ⇒ Id. None of these maps knows anything about the elements of X; each is built from the bare fact that X × X is a product.

The double dual, verified

Here is the example that started the subject. For a real vector space V, the dual V∗ is the space of linear maps V → ℝ. A linear map T : V → W induces a map backwards on duals, T∗ : W∗ → V∗, by composition: T∗(λ) = λ ∘ T. Dualizing twice gives a covariant functor V ↦ V∗∗, with T∗∗(φ) = φ ∘ T∗. Now define, for each V, the evaluation map

evV : V → V∗∗,     evV(v) = “evaluate at v”,  that is,  evV(v)(λ) = λ(v).

No basis was chosen. The claim is that ev : Id ⇒ (−)∗∗ is natural: for every linear T : V → W, we need T∗∗ ∘ evV = evW ∘ T. Both sides are elements of W∗∗ once fed a vector v, so test them on an arbitrary λ ∈ W∗:

T∗∗(evV(v))(λ) = evV(v)(T∗ λ) = evV(v)(λ ∘ T) = λ(T v) = evW(T v)(λ).

The square commutes. For finite-dimensional V each evV is an isomorphism, so a finite-dimensional vector space is naturally isomorphic to its double dual. With the single dual the story is different: V and V∗ have the same dimension, hence are isomorphic, but every isomorphism between them depends on a choice — and Exercise 10 proves that no choice-free one can exist.

A family that cannot be natural: choosing an element

To see what naturality rules out, try to choose one element from every non-empty set. Work in the category of non-empty sets and all functions between them, and let Δ be the constant functor at a one-element set {∗}. A natural transformation α : Δ ⇒ Id would consist of a function αX : {∗} → X for each X — that is, a chosen element aX ∈ X — such that for every function f : X → Y,

f(aX) = aY.

Take X = Y = {0, 1} and let f be the function exchanging 0 and 1. The condition demands f(aX) = aX, but f has no fixed point. So no such natural transformation exists: every way of choosing elements from all non-empty sets is disturbed by some function. This is the precise meaning of the informal phrase “there is no canonical choice” — and it is exactly how mathematicians use the word natural when they say a construction “involves no arbitrary choices.”

Maps between actions

Finally, an example where naturality reproduces a classical definition. In Lesson 2 a functor from a monoid M to Set was an action: a set S on which each m ∈ M acts by a function. Given two such functors, on sets S and S′, a natural transformation between them has just one component — a function α : S → S′ — and the naturality squares, one for each m, say

α(m · s) = m · α(s)     for all m and s.

Such a map is called equivariant: it commutes with the action. For the “clocks” of Lesson 2 — sets with a tick function t — the natural transformations are the functions with α ∘ t = t′ ∘ α, the maps that carry the motion of one system to the motion of the other. Natural transformations are the structure-preserving maps between functors, and here they recover exactly the structure-preserving maps of the classical theory.

§5 — The functor category

Natural transformations compose, so they too form a category

Given α : F ⇒ G and β : G ⇒ H, define their composite component-wise: (β ∘ α)X = βX ∘ αX, composing inside 𝒟. Is it natural? Draw the two naturality squares side by side, sharing the middle edge G f:

The identity natural transformation 1F has components idF X, and this composition is associative because composition in 𝒟 is. Recognize the pattern? It is the Lesson 1 definition of a category yet again. Fixing 𝒞 and 𝒟, the functors 𝒞 → 𝒟 are the objects and natural transformations are the arrows — a category, the functor category [𝒞, 𝒟].

And the loop closes neatly: an isomorphism in [𝒞, 𝒟] — in the Lesson 1 sense of an arrow with a two-sided inverse — is exactly a natural isomorphism, which by the lemma above is exactly a natural transformation whose components are all isos. Three vantage points, one notion.

One level higher, in passing

There is a second way to compose — horizontally, across different pairs of categories — and the two compositions satisfy an interchange law. That structure makes Cat a 2-category: objects, arrows between them, and arrows between those. We won’t need the machinery here; it’s enough to notice the tower keeps going up.

Four functor categories you already know

The construction is abstract, but small choices of 𝒞 give familiar things.

  • [𝟙, 𝒟] is 𝒟 itself: a functor out of 𝟙 is an object, and a natural transformation between two of them has a single component, an arrow.
  • If 𝒞 is the discrete category with two objects, [𝒞, 𝒟] is the product category 𝒟 × 𝒟 of Lesson 1, Exercise 5: a functor is a pair of objects, and — there being no arrows to impose naturality squares — a natural transformation is any pair of arrows.
  • [𝟚, 𝒟] is the arrow category of 𝒟: its objects are the arrows of 𝒟, and a morphism from the arrow f to the arrow g is a commuting square with f and g as opposite sides — the slogan of §7 below. Lesson 5 returns to it.
  • If M is a monoid viewed as a one-object category, [M, Set] is the category of M-actions and equivariant maps from §4.

Each of these was, or could have been, defined by hand. The functor category produces all of them from one definition.

§6 — Natural isomorphisms at work

“The same functor in disguise,” made exact

Lesson 2 kept saying that one functor was another “in disguise.” The natural isomorphism of §3 is what that phrase means.

Elements and pairs

Lesson 2 claimed that the hom functor Set(1, −) is the identity functor in disguise. Here is the natural isomorphism. For each set X, let

αX : Set(1, X) → X,     αX(h) = h(∗),

where ∗ is the single element of 1. Each αX is a bijection: a function out of a one-element set is determined by its one value, and any value will do. For naturality, take f : X → Y. The functor Set(1, −) sends f to “compose with f,” so the square asks whether αY(f ∘ h) = f(αX(h)). Both sides are f(h(∗)). So α is a natural isomorphism Set(1, −) ≅ Id.

The same argument with a two-element set gives Set(2, −) ≅ S, where S X = X × X is the squaring functor of §4: a function h on {0, 1} corresponds to the pair (h(0), h(1)), and composing with f corresponds to applying f to both entries.

“Natural in X”

Mathematicians rarely name the functors. They say instead: there is an isomorphism X × Y ≅ Y × X, natural in X and Y. The phrase has an exact meaning.

Definition · Natural in a variable

A family of isomorphisms F X ≅ G X, one for each object X, is natural in X when the isomorphisms are the components of a natural transformation between the functors F and G — that is, when they commute with F f and G f for every arrow f. When two variables are involved, “natural in both” means natural in each one with the other held fixed.

For the swap isomorphism σ : X × Y → Y × X, (x, y) ↦ (y, x), naturality in X says that for every f : X → X′, swapping and then applying f in the second slot equals applying f in the first slot and then swapping: both send (x, y) to (y, f(x)). This phrase will carry real weight in Lesson 8, where an adjunction is defined as a bijection “natural in both variables.”

What a natural isomorphism lets you transfer

Naturally isomorphic functors are indistinguishable for every purpose of category theory, in the same way that isomorphic objects are. For instance, if α : F ≅ G then for every arrow f : X → Y the naturality square can be solved for G f:

G f = αY ∘ F f ∘ αX−1.

So G f is F f conjugated by isomorphisms, and any property preserved by such conjugation passes from F to G: F f is an isomorphism exactly when G f is, and F is faithful or full exactly when G is (Exercise 16). Lesson 4 builds its central definition on this: two categories will count as “the same” when functors between them compose to the identity not on the nose, but up to natural isomorphism.

§7 — Naturality by hand

Finitely many squares, each checked in a line

When the source category is finite, a natural transformation is a finite list of components and naturality is a finite list of squares — one for each arrow of the source. Three worked examples, in increasing order of interest.

Between two arrows: a commuting square

A functor 𝟚 → 𝒟 picks out one arrow of 𝒟 (Lesson 2). So let F and G be two such functors, picking arrows f : A → B and g : C → D. What is a natural transformation α : F ⇒ G? It has two components, one for each object of 𝟚:

α0 : A → C    and    α1 : B → D.

There is a naturality square for each of the three arrows of 𝟚. The squares for the two identities say α0 = α0 and α1 = α1. The square for the one real arrow says

g ∘ α0 = α1 ∘ f.

So α is exactly a pair of arrows making a square commute. A natural transformation between two arrow-shaped diagrams is a commuting square — the general naturality square has itself as its smallest instance.

Between monotone maps: compare pointwise

Let F, G : 𝟚 → 𝟛 be functors between chains — monotone maps, as in Lesson 2. Since 𝟛 is thin, a component αx : F x → G x exists exactly when F x ≤ G x, there is at most one, and every square commutes for free. So there is one natural transformation F ⇒ G if F ≤ G pointwise, and none otherwise. With F = (0, 1) and G = (1, 2): yes, since 0 ≤ 1 and 1 ≤ 2. With F = (0, 2) and G = (1, 1): no, since 2 ≤ 1 fails at the second object. The functor category [𝟚, 𝟛] is therefore itself a poset: the six monotone maps of Lesson 2, ordered pointwise.

Between actions: count the equivariant maps

Now a case where the count is neither 0 nor 1. A functor from the group ℤ/2 to Set is a set with an involution (Lesson 2, Exercise 8). Take

  • S = {1, 2, 3}, with the involution s exchanging 1 and 2 and fixing 3;
  • S′ = {a, b}, with s′ exchanging a and b;
  • S″ = {a, b, c}, with s″ exchanging a and b and fixing c.

A natural transformation is a function α with α ∘ s = s′ ∘ α (§4). From S to S′: the point 3 is fixed by s, so α(3) = α(s(3)) = s′(α(3)) — the value α(3) would have to be a fixed point of s′, and s′ has none. There are no natural transformations. From S to S″: now α(3) must be the fixed point c. The value α(1) can be any of a, b, c, and then α(2) = α(s(1)) = s″(α(1)) is forced. So there are exactly three. Naturality is a real constraint — it cut 23 = 8 functions down to none, and 33 = 27 functions down to three — but not always a fatal one.

Optional · PythonNaturality, checked by machine

First, the structural examples. We confirm the naturality square for reverse (a transformation List ⇒ List) and for safe_head (a transformation List ⇒ Maybe, with Maybe modelled as a list of length 0 or 1).

natural.py — polymorphic functions are natural
def fmap(f, xs):                 # the List functor on arrows
    return [f(x) for x in xs]

def reverse(xs):                 # the component of  reverse : List => List
    return xs[::-1]

inc  = lambda x: x + 1
data = [1, 2, 3, 4]

# Naturality of reverse:   map f . reverse  ==  reverse . map f
assert fmap(inc, reverse(data)) == reverse(fmap(inc, data))
print(fmap(inc, reverse(data)))              # [5, 4, 3, 2]

# safe_head : List => Maybe   (Maybe modelled as a 0-or-1 element list)
def safe_head(xs):
    return [xs[0]] if xs else []

# Naturality of safe_head holds for every list and every f
for xs in ([], [10, 20, 30]):
    assert fmap(inc, safe_head(xs)) == safe_head(fmap(inc, xs))
print("reverse and safe_head are natural")

Now the finite version, reusing FiniteCategory and Functor from the previous lessons — repeated in the setup block below, together with the two small categories the demonstration needs. A natural transformation is one component arrow per object; check() verifies the typing and the naturality square for every arrow. We use the cleanest possible illustration: two functors out of 𝟚 pick two opposite edges of a commuting square, and a natural transformation between them is that commuting square — its components are the other two edges.

setup.py — carried over from Lessons 1 and 2, plus two small categories
from itertools import product

class FiniteCategory:
    """objects, arrows (name -> (dom, cod)), a composition table,
       and a chosen identity arrow for each object."""
    def __init__(self, objects, arrows, comp, ident):
        self.obj, self.ar = set(objects), arrows
        self.comp, self.id = comp, ident

    def dom(self, a): return self.ar[a][0]
    def cod(self, a): return self.ar[a][1]
    def composable(self, g, f): return self.dom(g) == self.cod(f)

    def check(self):
        # 1. each identity is typed X -> X
        for X in self.obj:
            assert self.ar[self.id[X]] == (X, X)
        # 2. every composable pair has a composite, correctly typed
        for g, f in product(self.ar, repeat=2):
            if self.composable(g, f):
                gf = self.comp[(g, f)]
                assert self.ar[gf] == (self.dom(f), self.cod(g))
        # 3. identity law on both sides
        for f in self.ar:
            X, Y = self.ar[f]
            assert self.comp[(self.id[Y], f)] == f
            assert self.comp[(f, self.id[X])] == f
        # 4. associativity
        for h, g, f in product(self.ar, repeat=3):
            if self.composable(h, g) and self.composable(g, f):
                assert self.comp[(h, self.comp[(g, f)])] == \
                       self.comp[(self.comp[(h, g)], f)]
        return True

class Functor:
    """F : C -> D, given by a map on objects and a map on arrows."""
    def __init__(self, C, D, on_obj, on_arr):
        self.C, self.D, self.on_obj, self.on_arr = C, D, on_obj, on_arr

    def check(self):
        C, D, Fo, Fa = self.C, self.D, self.on_obj, self.on_arr
        for f in C.ar:                        # typing: F f : F X -> F Y
            X, Y = C.ar[f]
            assert D.ar[Fa[f]] == (Fo[X], Fo[Y])
        for X in C.obj:                       # preserves identities
            assert Fa[C.id[X]] == D.id[Fo[X]]
        for g, f in product(C.ar, repeat=2):  # preserves composition
            if C.composable(g, f):
                assert Fa[C.comp[(g, f)]] == D.comp[(Fa[g], Fa[f])]
        return True

# the category 2 = ( 0 --u--> 1 )
two_ar   = {'i0':(0,0), 'i1':(1,1), 'u':(0,1)}
two_comp = {('i0','i0'):'i0', ('i1','i1'):'i1',
            ('u','i0'):'u',  ('i1','u'):'u'}
Two = FiniteCategory({0,1}, two_ar, two_comp, {0:'i0', 1:'i1'})

# the commuting square:  f: A->B, p: A->C, q: B->D, g: C->D,  d = q.f = g.p
arr = {'iA':('A','A'), 'iB':('B','B'), 'iC':('C','C'), 'iD':('D','D'),
       'f':('A','B'), 'p':('A','C'), 'q':('B','D'), 'g':('C','D'),
       'd':('A','D')}
ident = {'A':'iA', 'B':'iB', 'C':'iC', 'D':'iD'}
comp = {}
for x, (u, v) in arr.items():
    comp[(ident[v], x)] = x          # id . x = x
    comp[(x, ident[u])] = x          # x . id = x
comp[('q','f')] = 'd'                # q . f = d
comp[('g','p')] = 'd'                # g . p = d
Square = FiniteCategory(set('ABCD'), arr, comp, ident)
assert Square.check() and Two.check()
nat_transformation.py — a naturality checker
class Nat:
    """alpha : F => G, with F, G : C -> D sharing source and target.
       `comp` maps each object X of C to a component arrow alpha_X of D."""
    def __init__(self, F, G, comp):
        self.F, self.G, self.comp = F, G, comp

    def check(self):
        F, G, C, D = self.F, self.G, self.F.C, self.F.D
        for X in C.obj:           # component typing:  alpha_X : F X -> G X
            assert D.ar[self.comp[X]] == (F.on_obj[X], G.on_obj[X])
        for f in C.ar:            # naturality:  G f . alpha_X == alpha_Y . F f
            X, Y = C.ar[f]
            assert D.comp[(G.on_arr[f], self.comp[X])] == \
                   D.comp[(self.comp[Y], F.on_arr[f])]
        return True

# F and G send the category 2 to two opposite edges of the square; a natural
# transformation between them is EXACTLY a commuting square (components p, q).
F = Functor(Two, Square, {0:'A', 1:'B'}, {'i0':'iA', 'i1':'iB', 'u':'f'})
G = Functor(Two, Square, {0:'C', 1:'D'}, {'i0':'iC', 'i1':'iD', 'u':'g'})
alpha = Nat(F, G, comp={0:'p', 1:'q'})
print(alpha.check())

The mathematical examples of §4 can be checked the same way. Singletons and unions are natural for the powerset functor; and a brute-force search confirms the count of equivariant maps found by hand above.

more_naturality.py — the examples of §4 and §7, by brute force
from itertools import combinations, product

def subsets(X):
    X = list(X)
    return [frozenset(c) for r in range(len(X) + 1)
                         for c in combinations(X, r)]

P     = lambda f: (lambda S: frozenset(f(x) for x in S))   # direct image
eta   = lambda x: frozenset([x])                           # singleton
union = lambda SS: frozenset(x for S in SS for x in S)

X = {1, 2, 3}
f = {1: 'a', 2: 'b', 3: 'a'}.get
families = subsets(subsets(X))            # all 256 sets of subsets of X
assert all(P(f)(eta(x)) == eta(f(x)) for x in X)
assert all(P(f)(union(SS)) == union(P(P(f))(SS)) for SS in families)
print("singleton and union are natural;", len(families), "families checked")

def equivariant(s, t):
    "all functions a with  a . s == t . a,  for involutions s and t"
    S, T = sorted(s), sorted(t)
    maps = [dict(zip(S, vals)) for vals in product(T, repeat=len(S))]
    return [a for a in maps if all(a[s[x]] == t[a[x]] for x in S)]

s  = {1: 2, 2: 1, 3: 3}                   # on S   = {1, 2, 3}
s1 = {'a': 'b', 'b': 'a'}                 # on S'  = {a, b}
s2 = {'a': 'b', 'b': 'a', 'c': 'c'}       # on S'' = {a, b, c}
print("S -> S' :", len(equivariant(s, s1)), "equivariant maps")
print("S -> S'':", len(equivariant(s, s2)), "equivariant maps")

Going deeper

Horizontal composition and the interchange law

Exercise 17’s whiskering — pushing a natural transformation through a functor — is half of a richer operation. Given α : F ⇒ G between functors 𝒞 → 𝒟 and β : H ⇒ K between functors 𝒟 → ℰ, there is a horizontal composite β ∗ α : H∘F ⇒ K∘G — a transformation between the composite functors, built from one transformation on each floor. Its component at X can be written two ways: cross with β first and then translate α through K, or translate first and cross second. Exercise 18 shows one naturality square makes the two routes agree.

Vertical composition (§5) and horizontal composition coexist under a compatibility equation — the interchange law, (β′ ∘ β) ∗ (α′ ∘ α) = (β′ ∗ α′) ∘ (β ∗ α) — and the full package (categories, functors, natural transformations, two compositions, interchange) is what “Cat is a 2-category,” the aside of §5, actually means. None of the rest of this course needs the general machinery, but it is worth having seen the doorway: everything in these lessons is the ground floor of a taller building.

Whiskering on either side

Exercise 17 pushes a natural transformation forward through a functor. There is a companion operation that pulls one back, and both will be needed in Lesson 10. Let α : F ⇒ G be a natural transformation between functors 𝒞 → 𝒟.

  • If H : 𝒟 → ℰ is a functor, then Hα : H ∘ F ⇒ H ∘ G has components (Hα)X = H(αX). Apply H to each component.
  • If K : ℬ → 𝒞 is a functor, then αK : F ∘ K ⇒ G ∘ K has components (αK)W = αK W. Nothing is applied: one simply uses the components of α at the objects in the image of K.

The second kind is natural for an even simpler reason than the first: its naturality square at an arrow w of ℬ is the naturality square of α at the arrow K w. Whiskering on both sides at once, H α K, has components H(αK W); in Lesson 10 the multiplication of a monad is built exactly this way.

§8 — Exercises

Eighteen problems on naturality

Naturality proofs are short but the squares must be drawn with care — when in doubt, draw the square first and only then write the equation. The problems are grouped by topic, following the lesson’s sections, and within each group they run from easier to harder. Every problem can be done by hand; Exercises 3, 4 and 13 also carry optional Python, reusing fmap and the Nat checker from the lab in §7.

Naturality for lists

§2–§3 · Exercises 1–5

Exercise 1warm-up

Instantiate the naturality square for reverse with the concrete function upper, which turns a letter into its capital, and the concrete list ['a', 'b'].

Compute both paths — across-then-down and down-then-across — element by element, and confirm they agree. Then say in one sentence why they were bound to agree.

Show solution

Across then down: reverse(['a','b']) = ['b','a'], then map(upper) gives ['B','A']. Down then across: map(upper)(['a','b']) = ['A','B'], then reverse gives ['B','A']. Both paths land on ['B','A'].

They had to: reverse only permutes positions and upper only transforms contents, and the two concerns cannot interact. That separation of concerns is naturality, seen from ground level.

Exercise 2examples

Decide, with justification, whether each of the following is a natural transformation, and if so between which functors:

  1. double, which concatenates a list with itself: [1, 2] ↦ [1, 2, 1, 2].
  2. total, which replaces a list of numbers by the sum of its elements, proposed as a transformation List ⇒ List.
Show solution

(a) Natural, List ⇒ List. double duplicates and rearranges positions without inspecting elements, so map f (xs + xs) = (map f xs) + (map f xs) for every f — the square commutes.

(b) Not even well-typed as stated. The output of total is a number, not a list, so its components do not have the type List X → List X; and worse, it only exists at numeric element types, whereas a transformation List ⇒ List must supply a component at every type. (A cousin of the idea does work: length : List ⇒ Const ℕ is natural, because length is blind to contents.) The moral: naturality is a property of a family relative to a declared pair of functors — you must say what the source and target are before the question even makes sense.

Exercise 3by hand · optional python

Not every function of type “list to list” is natural — only the ones blind to their elements. Test two candidates, by hand on small lists:

  1. duplicate, which repeats each element in place: [1,2] ↦ [1,1,2,2].
  2. sorted, which puts a list of numbers into increasing order, proposed as a transformation List ⇒ List.

For each candidate t, check the naturality square — map f over the list and then apply t, or apply t and then map f — on well-chosen inputs. For sorted, find an explicit counterexample and explain what property of sorting is to blame.

Optional · Python

Define duplicate = lambda xs: [x for x in xs for _ in (0, 1)], take Python’s built-in sorted for the second candidate, and test the square fmap(f, t(xs)) == t(fmap(f, xs)) with the lab’s fmap.

Show solution

duplicate is natural — it repeats positions without reading values, so the square commutes for every f and every list.

sorted is not. Take the list [1, 2] and let f be negation, x ↦ −x. One path: sorted([1,2]) = [1,2], then map(f) = [-1,-2]. Other path: map(f) = [-1,-2], then sorted = [-2,-1]. The results differ.

Sorting compares elements — it reads their values to decide the output order — and a non-monotone f like negation changes the verdicts of those comparisons. Blindness to contents is not a stylistic nicety; it is exactly the property the naturality square detects.

Exercise 4by hand · optional python

Two more candidates for naturality, one of each kind:

  1. take2, which keeps the first two elements of a list (fewer if the list is short).
  2. dedupe, which removes duplicates, keeping first occurrences: [1, 2, 1] ↦ [1, 2].

Test each against the naturality square. For the failure, find a counterexample using a non-injective function, and explain what operation on elements the failure exposes. (Note the contrast with Exercise 3: sorted failed via a non-monotone map. Different inspections, different witnesses.)

Optional · Python

In Python the two candidates are take2 = lambda xs: xs[:2] and dedupe = lambda xs: list(dict.fromkeys(xs)). Test the square for each with the lab’s fmap, and print a counterexample for the one that fails.

Show solution

take2 is natural: it selects positions 0 and 1 without reading values, so mapping and truncating commute for every function and list.

dedupe is not — it tests elements for equality, and a non-injective map manufactures new equalities. Counterexample: take the list [1, 2] and the constant function f(x) = 0. Removing duplicates first changes nothing, and mapping then gives [0, 0]. Mapping first gives [0, 0], and removing duplicates then leaves [0].

Optional · Python

On one path the two elements are distinct when dedupe looks, and both survive; on the other they have already been merged, and one is deleted. Equality-testing is content-inspection just as much as comparison is — naturality is a fine-grained instrument, and each failure mode names the exact operation that broke it.

Exercise 5examples

Consider flatten (often called concat or join), which turns a list of lists into a single list: flatten([[1,2],[3]]) = [1,2,3].

It is a natural transformation — but between which two functors? State the source and target carefully, write down the naturality square, and verify it on the list of lists xss = [[1,2],[3]], with f the function “multiply by 10.”

Show solution

The source is the composite functor List ∘ List (lists of lists) and the target is List: flatten : List∘List ⇒ List. The naturality square reads map f (flatten xss) == flatten (map (map f) xss) — note the double map on the right, because the composite functor acts on arrows by map(map f).

On the example: left path, flatten = [1,2,3] then map(*10) = [10,20,30]; right path, map(map(*10)) = [[10,20],[30]] then flatten = [10,20,30]. Equal. flatten is another positions-only operation — it erases inner brackets and touches no elements. (File this one away: flatten is the multiplication of the List monad, and its naturality is part of what makes List a monad, as Lesson 10 explains.)

Naturality in mathematics

§4 · Exercises 6–10

Exercise 6structure

Let 𝒞 and 𝒟 be preorders viewed as categories, so that functors F, G : 𝒞 → 𝒟 are monotone maps (Lesson 2, §4).

What does a natural transformation F ⇒ G amount to, concretely? How much of the definition survives, and how much becomes automatic?

Show solution

A component αX : F X → G X is an arrow of the preorder 𝒟, which exists precisely when F X ≤ G X — and when it exists it is unique. The naturality squares then commute for free, because preorders are thin (Lesson 1, Exercise 7: every diagram in a thin category commutes).

So a natural transformation between monotone maps is exactly the statement F X ≤ G X for every X — the pointwise order on monotone functions. All the data evaporates; only the condition remains. Thin categories keep doing this: they are the shadow of category theory where every “how” collapses to a “whether.”

Exercise 7by hand

Test three families of maps on the powerset functor P of §4, using the function f : {1, 2, 3} → {a, b} with f(1) = f(3) = a and f(2) = b.

  1. Singletons: check the naturality square of η at the element 3.
  2. Unions: check the naturality square of μ at the family { {1, 2}, {3} }.
  3. Intersections: let νX send a non-empty family of subsets to its intersection. Show the square for ν fails at the family { {1}, {3} }, and say which property of f is to blame.
Show solution

(a) Across then down: 3 ↦ {3} ↦ f({3}) = {a}. Down then across: 3 ↦ f(3) = a ↦ {a}. Equal.

(b) Union first: {1, 2} ∪ {3} = {1, 2, 3}, with direct image {a, b}. Images first: the family becomes { {a, b}, {a} }, with union {a, b}. Equal.

(c) Intersection first: {1} ∩ {3} = ∅, with direct image ∅. Images first: both members become {a}, and the intersection is {a}. Not equal — so intersection is not a natural transformation P ∘ P ⇒ P. The culprit is that f is not injective: it merges 1 and 3, creating a common element that the original sets did not share. Direct images preserve unions but not intersections; naturality detects the difference.

Exercise 8examples

Clocks (§4). Let Cn be the set {0, 1, …, n − 1} with the tick t(i) = i + 1 modulo n — a functor from (ℕ, +) to Set. A natural transformation Cn ⇒ Cm is a function α with α ∘ t = t ∘ α.

  1. Show there is no natural transformation C3 ⇒ C2.
  2. Find all natural transformations C6 ⇒ C2.
  3. For which n, m does a natural transformation Cn ⇒ Cm exist, and how many are there?
Show solution

The key observation: since i is reached from 0 by i ticks, equivariance forces α(i) = α(0) + i modulo m. So α is determined by the single value α(0). But n ticks return 0 to itself in Cn, so we also need α(0) = α(0) + n modulo m — that is, m must divide n.

(a) 2 does not divide 3: none.

(b) 2 divides 6, and α(0) may be 0 or 1: two natural transformations, α(i) = i mod 2 and α(i) = i + 1 mod 2.

(c) They exist exactly when m divides n, and then there are m of them, one for each choice of α(0). A slow clock can drive a fast one only when the periods are compatible — naturality here is ordinary arithmetic.

Exercise 9arrows-only

Show that the only natural transformation from the identity functor on Set to itself is the identity.

Hint: for an element x of a set X, consider the function h : 1 → X from a one-element set that picks out x, and write down the naturality square at h.

Show solution

Let α : Id ⇒ Id be natural, and let h : 1 → X send the single element ∗ to x. Naturality at the arrow h says αX ∘ h = h ∘ α1. But α1 is a function from a one-element set to itself, and there is only one such function, the identity. So αX ∘ h = h, and evaluating at ∗ gives αX(x) = x. Since x and X were arbitrary, every component of α is an identity.

The argument has two ingredients: the object 1 is rigid (it has no symmetries), and arrows out of 1 reach every element of every set. A family of maps that must commute with all of those probes has no freedom left. Lesson 9 turns this proof technique into the Yoneda lemma.

Exercise 10think

No natural isomorphism V ≅ V∗. The dual is contravariant, so to compare it with the identity functor it must first be made covariant. Work in the category of finite-dimensional real vector spaces and invertible linear maps, and define D(V) = V∗ and, for an invertible T : V → W, D(T) = (T∗)−1 : V∗ → W∗.

  1. Check that D is a (covariant) functor on this category.
  2. Suppose φ were a natural transformation from the identity functor to D. Write out its naturality square at an invertible map T : V → V.
  3. Take T to be multiplication by 2, and conclude that φV = 0 — so φV is not an isomorphism unless V is the zero space.
Show solution

(a) D(id) = (id∗)−1 = id. For composites, recall (T ∘ S)∗ = S∗ ∘ T∗, so D(T ∘ S) = (S∗ ∘ T∗)−1 = (T∗)−1 ∘ (S∗)−1 = D(T) ∘ D(S). Two reversals — one from dualizing, one from inverting — cancel, leaving a covariant functor.

(b) D(T) ∘ φV = φV ∘ T, as maps V → V∗.

(c) If T = 2 · idV then T∗(λ) = λ ∘ T = 2λ, so T∗ is also multiplication by 2 and D(T) is multiplication by ½. The square reads ½ φV = 2 φV, hence φV = 0.

So no choice of isomorphisms V ≅ V∗ is compatible with even the simplest symmetries of V, the rescalings. Vectors and linear functionals transform in opposite ways under a change of scale — physicists call them contravariant and covariant vectors for exactly this reason — and that mismatch is what the failure of naturality records. The double dual escapes it because two reversals cancel.

The functor category

§5, §7 · Exercises 11–14

Exercise 11arrows-only

For a functor F, define the family αX = idF X, one identity arrow per object.

Prove this family is a natural transformation F ⇒ F. (It is the identity arrow on F in the functor category — so this exercise checks that the functor category has its identities.)

Show solution

Fix any f : X → Y. The naturality square demands F f ∘ αX = αY ∘ F f, i.e. F f ∘ idF X = idF Y ∘ F f. By the identity law of the target category, both sides are just F f. Every square commutes, so 1F is natural.

Together with Exercise 12 below, this confirms the functor category [𝒞, 𝒟] really is a category in the Lesson 1 sense: identities exist, composites exist, and the laws are inherited pointwise from 𝒟.

Exercise 12arrows-only

Let α : F ⇒ G and β : G ⇒ H be natural transformations, and define the composite componentwise: (β ∘ α)X = βX ∘ αX.

Prove that β ∘ α is natural, and explain how your algebra corresponds to the picture of gluing two naturality squares along their shared middle edge.

Show solution

Fix f : X → Y and compute H f ∘ (βX ∘ αX). Re-associate and apply β’s naturality square (H f ∘ βX = βY ∘ G f) to get βY ∘ (G f ∘ αX); now apply α’s square (G f ∘ αX = αY ∘ F f) to reach (βY ∘ αY) ∘ F f. That is exactly the naturality square for β ∘ α.

Each application of a naturality square is one “pane” of the §5 picture: the first rewrite carries the path across the right square, the second across the left. Two commuting panes glued along the shared column G f make the outer rectangle commute — the algebra and the picture are the same proof.

Exercise 13by hand · optional python

In the commuting square of §7, let F and G be the functors 𝟚 → 𝒟 picking out the arrows f : A → B and g : C → D, where the square’s other two sides are p : A → C and q : B → D and these nine arrows are all there are (Lesson 1, Exercise 12).

  1. Someone proposes the components α0 = p and α1 = f. Without checking any square, explain why this is not a natural transformation F ⇒ G.
  2. How many natural transformations F ⇒ G are there? How many G ⇒ F?

Exercise 14structure

Work in the functor category [𝟚, 𝒟], whose objects are the arrows of 𝒟 and whose morphisms are commuting squares (§5).

  1. Using the lemma of §3, describe the isomorphisms of [𝟚, 𝒟].
  2. In [𝟚, Set], consider three functions {1, 2} → {a, b}: f constant at a, g constant at b, and the bijection h with 1 ↦ a, 2 ↦ b. Which of them are isomorphic to which?
Show solution

(a) An isomorphism in a functor category is a natural transformation all of whose components are isomorphisms. So an isomorphism from f : A → B to g : C → D is a commuting square g ∘ α0 = α1 ∘ f in which both α0 : A → C and α1 : B → D are isomorphisms. Equivalently g = α1 ∘ f ∘ α0−1: the arrow g is f with its source and target relabelled.

(b) f ≅ g: take α0 the identity and α1 the swap of a and b; then α1 ∘ f is constant at b, which is g. But h is isomorphic to neither: if h = α1 ∘ f ∘ α0−1 with f constant, then h would be constant, and it is not. Isomorphism in the arrow category respects every arrows-only feature of a function — here, being a bijection.

Natural isomorphisms

§6 · Exercises 15–16

Exercise 15by hand

Lesson 2 observed that a function X → 2, where 2 = {0, 1}, is the same thing as a subset of X. Make this a natural isomorphism between the two contravariant functors Set(−, 2) and “inverse-image powerset.”

  1. Write down the component at X and its inverse.
  2. State the naturality square for a function f : X → Y — taking care that both functors reverse f — and verify it.
Show solution

(a) Send a function χ : X → 2 to the subset χ−1(1) = { x : χ(x) = 1 }. The inverse sends a subset T to its characteristic function, which is 1 on T and 0 elsewhere.

(b) Both functors turn f : X → Y into a map from the Y-side to the X-side: Set(−, 2) sends χ : Y → 2 to χ ∘ f, and the powerset functor sends T ⊆ Y to f−1(T). The square asks that, starting from χ : Y → 2,

(χ ∘ f)−1(1) = f−1(χ−1(1)).

It holds: x lies in the left side exactly when χ(f(x)) = 1, and in the right side exactly when f(x) ∈ χ−1(1) — the same condition. So the powerset functor is the hom functor into 2, up to natural isomorphism: in the language of Lesson 9, it is represented by the two-element set.

Exercise 16arrows-only

Let α : F ⇒ G be a natural isomorphism between functors 𝒞 → 𝒟. Using the formula G f = αY ∘ F f ∘ αX−1 of §6, prove:

  1. F f is an isomorphism if and only if G f is.
  2. F is faithful if and only if G is, and F is full if and only if G is.
Show solution

(a) If F f is an isomorphism then G f is a composite of three isomorphisms. Conversely F f = αY−1 ∘ G f ∘ αX, by solving the formula for F f.

(b) Fix objects X, Y. The function k ↦ αY ∘ k ∘ αX−1 is a bijection from 𝒟(F X, F Y) to 𝒟(G X, G Y), with inverse k′ ↦ αY−1 ∘ k′ ∘ αX. The formula says that the hom-set function of G is the hom-set function of F followed by this bijection:

𝒞(X, Y) → 𝒟(F X, F Y) → 𝒟(G X, G Y).

Following an injective function by a bijection gives an injective function, and likewise for surjective. So G is faithful (or full) exactly when F is.

Category theory never needs to distinguish naturally isomorphic functors, and this is why: every property worth stating survives the conjugation.

Whiskering and horizontal composition

Going deeper · Exercises 17–18

Exercise 17arrows-only

Natural transformations can be pushed forward through a functor. Let α : F ⇒ G be natural, with F, G : 𝒞 → 𝒟, and let H : 𝒟 → ℰ be any functor.

Show that the family with components H(αX) is a natural transformation H∘F ⇒ H∘G. (This operation is called whiskering; it is half of the horizontal composition mentioned in §5’s aside.)

Show solution

Take the naturality square of α at f : X → Y, which is the equation G f ∘ αX = αY ∘ F f, and apply the functor H to both sides. Composition-preservation splits each side: H(G f) ∘ H(αX) = H(αY) ∘ H(F f).

Reading H(G f) as (H∘G)(f) and H(F f) as (H∘F)(f), this is exactly the naturality square for the family H(αX) between the composite functors. The proof is one move — “apply H and use that it preserves composition” — and it is a template: functors transport not just objects, arrows, and diagrams, but entire natural transformations.

Exercise 18arrows-only

With α : F ⇒ G (functors 𝒞 → 𝒟) and β : H ⇒ K (functors 𝒟 → ℰ), the two candidate formulas for the horizontal composite’s component at X are:

K(αX) ∘ βF X   and   βG X ∘ H(αX).

Show they are equal. Hint: αX is an arrow of 𝒟 — at which arrow should you instantiate the naturality of β?

Show solution

Instantiate β’s naturality square at the arrow αX : F X → G X of 𝒟. The square reads, verbatim: K(αX) ∘ βF X = βG X ∘ H(αX). That is the required equation — no computation left to do.

The pleasure of this proof is realizing that a component of one transformation is a perfectly ordinary arrow, eligible to be fed into the naturality of another. Layers feeding layers is the everyday texture of the subject from here on: Lesson 10 builds monad multiplication by exactly this kind of move (whiskering a counit), and its associativity proof instantiates a naturality square at a component, just as here.

Recap

What to carry forward

  1. A natural transformation α : F ⇒ G is one component arrow αX : F X → G X per object, such that every naturality square G f ∘ αX = αY ∘ F f commutes.
  2. Naturality is uniformity: a family that commutes with the action of every arrow. Parametrically polymorphic functions are natural for free.
  3. A natural isomorphism (every component an iso) is the right notion of two functors being “the same” — the distinction “iso vs. naturally iso” is why the field was founded.
  4. Natural transformations compose by gluing squares, so functors 𝒞 → 𝒟 form the functor category [𝒞, 𝒟]; its isomorphisms are exactly the natural isos.
  5. Slogan to keep: a natural transformation between two arrow-shaped diagrams is a commuting square.
  6. Naturality is everywhere in mathematics — singletons and unions, diagonals, the double dual, equivariant maps — and it is a real constraint: there is no natural way to choose an element of every set, and no natural isomorphism V ≅ V∗.
  7. “An isomorphism natural in X” means: the components of a natural isomorphism of functors. Naturally isomorphic functors share every categorical property.
