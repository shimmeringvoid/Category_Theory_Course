Equivalence of Categories

Isomorphic categories are rare; equivalent ones are everywhere. The right notion of sameness for categories is sameness up to natural isomorphism — and it comes with a three-part test.

§1 — The question

When are two categories the same?

Lesson 1 taught a habit: never ask whether two objects are equal, only whether they are isomorphic. Lesson 3 applied the habit one floor up: two functors are “the same” when they are naturally isomorphic. This lesson climbs the last step. Categories are themselves objects — of the category Cat from Lesson 2 — so when should two categories be considered the same?

The definitions so far hand us an answer for free. An isomorphism of categories is an isomorphism in Cat: a pair of functors F : 𝒞 → 𝒟 and G : 𝒟 → 𝒞 with

G ∘ F = Id𝒞    and    F ∘ G = Id𝒟.

Sometimes this is exactly right. Reversing the arrows of a category twice gives back the category: (𝒞op)op = 𝒞. The category of relations is isomorphic to its own opposite, by sending each relation to its converse (Lesson 1, Exercise 19). But as a general notion of sameness, isomorphism of categories fails — it is far too strict. Three examples make the point.

  • Finite sets. Let FinSet be the category of all finite sets and all functions between them. Let Fin be the much smaller category whose only objects are the standard finite sets [0] = ∅, [1] = {1}, [2] = {1, 2}, [3] = {1, 2, 3}, and so on, again with all functions. Anyone doing combinatorics moves between the two without a thought: every finite set can be numbered. Yet they are not isomorphic. Fin has one object with three elements; FinSet has one for every three-element set there is.
  • Linear algebra. The category Mat of Lesson 1 has the numbers 0, 1, 2, … as objects and matrices as arrows. The category FinVect has all finite-dimensional real vector spaces as objects and linear maps as arrows. A first course in linear algebra is largely the discovery that these are two languages for one subject. But Mat has countably many objects, and FinVect far more.
  • The smallest case. Let 𝟙 be the category with one object and its identity, and let ℐ be the category with two objects a and b and, besides the identities, one arrow u : a → b and its inverse u−1 : b → a. In ℐ the two objects are isomorphic, so by Lesson 1 they are interchangeable — ℐ contains one object, twice. But a category with two objects cannot be isomorphic to a category with one.

The diagnosis is simple. The equation G ∘ F = Id says that G(F X) equals X for every object X. That is an equality between objects — precisely the kind of statement this course has learned not to make. The definition of isomorphism of categories breaks the habit of Lesson 1. Repair it, by asking only that G(F X) be isomorphic to X, in a way that is natural in X, and the right notion appears.

§2 — The definition

The same, up to natural isomorphism

Definition · Equivalence of categories

An equivalence between categories 𝒞 and 𝒟 consists of functors F : 𝒞 → 𝒟 and G : 𝒟 → 𝒞 together with natural isomorphisms

η : Id𝒞 ≅ G ∘ F    and    ε : F ∘ G ≅ Id𝒟.

When such data exist, 𝒞 and 𝒟 are equivalent, written 𝒞 ≃ 𝒟. One also says that the functor F is an equivalence, and calls G a pseudo-inverse of F.

Unwind the definition. For every object X of 𝒞 there is an isomorphism ηX : X → G F X, and these fit together naturally: for every arrow f : X → X′ the square G F f ∘ ηX = ηX′ ∘ f commutes. Likewise every object Y of 𝒟 comes with an isomorphism εY : F G Y → Y, natural in Y. A round trip does not return you to the object you started from — only to an isomorphic one, and the isomorphism is part of the data.

Two immediate remarks. First, an isomorphism of categories is an equivalence: take η and ε to be identity transformations. Second, the definition is symmetric: if (F, G, η, ε) is an equivalence from 𝒞 to 𝒟, then (G, F, ε−1, η−1) is one from 𝒟 to 𝒞, because the inverse of a natural isomorphism is natural (the lemma of Lesson 3). So G is an equivalence too.

The smallest equivalence

The categories 𝟙 and ℐ of §1 are equivalent, and seeing why takes the mystery out of the definition. Let F : 𝟙 → ℐ pick out the object a, and let G : ℐ → 𝟙 be the only functor there is. Then G ∘ F is exactly the identity of 𝟙, so η can be the identity. The other composite F ∘ G is not the identity of ℐ: it sends both a and b to a, and every arrow to ida. But it is naturally isomorphic to the identity. Define

εa = ida : a → a    and    εb = u : a → b.

Both components are isomorphisms, as required. Naturality must be checked at each arrow of ℐ; at the arrow u it reads u ∘ εa = εb ∘ ida, which says u = u. §7 carries out the remaining checks. So 𝟙 ≃ ℐ: the second object of ℐ costs nothing, because the isomorphism u is there to pay for it.

§3 — Composing equivalences

Equivalence is an equivalence relation

A notion of sameness ought to be reflexive, symmetric and transitive. The first two were observed above. The third needs the whiskering of Lesson 3.

Proposition · Equivalences compose

If 𝒞 ≃ 𝒟 and 𝒟 ≃ ℰ, then 𝒞 ≃ ℰ.

Proof. Let (F, G, η, ε) be an equivalence between 𝒞 and 𝒟, and (F′, G′, η′, ε′) one between 𝒟 and ℰ. The functors of the composite equivalence are F′ ∘ F : 𝒞 → ℰ and G ∘ G′ : ℰ → 𝒞. For its first natural isomorphism, take at each object X the composite

X → G F X → G G′ F′ F X,

where the first arrow is ηX and the second is G(η′F X). The first is a component of the natural isomorphism η. The second is a component of η′ whiskered by F on one side and G on the other — natural by Lesson 3, and an isomorphism because functors preserve isomorphisms. A composite of natural isomorphisms is a natural isomorphism, so this is one from Id𝒞 to (G ∘ G′) ∘ (F′ ∘ F). The second natural isomorphism is built the same way, as the composite F′ F G G′ Z → F′ G′ Z → Z of F′(εG′ Z) followed by ε′Z. ∎

So categories fall into equivalence classes, just as objects of a category fall into isomorphism classes. The rest of the lesson is about recognising when two categories are in the same class — ideally without having to construct G, η and ε at all.

§4 — The recognition theorem

Full, faithful, essentially surjective

Lesson 2 introduced three properties of a functor — faithful, full, essentially surjective — and promised that together they mean nothing is lost. Here is the promise kept. It is the most used theorem about equivalences, because it lets you recognise one by inspecting a single functor.

Theorem · Characterisation of equivalences

A functor F : 𝒞 → 𝒟 is an equivalence if and only if it is full, faithful and essentially surjective.

The proof has two halves of very different character. The first extracts three properties from given data. The second builds a pseudo-inverse from nothing but the three properties.

Proof, first half: an equivalence has the three properties. Let (F, G, η, ε) be an equivalence.

Essentially surjective. Every object Y of 𝒟 is isomorphic to F(G Y), by εY.

Faithful. For any arrow f : X → X′, the naturality square of η reads G F f ∘ ηX = ηX′ ∘ f, and since ηX′ is invertible it can be solved for f:

f = ηX′−1 ∘ G F f ∘ ηX.

So f can be recovered from F f. If F f = F f′ then the right-hand sides agree, and f = f′. By the symmetry noted in §2, the same argument shows that G is faithful.

Full. Let k : F X → F X′ be any arrow of 𝒟. We need an f with F f = k, and the formula above suggests what to try: let f = ηX′−1 ∘ G k ∘ ηX. Then, using the naturality square once more,

G F f = ηX′ ∘ f ∘ ηX−1 = G k.

So G(F f) = G(k), and G is faithful: F f = k.

Second half: the three properties give an equivalence. Let F be full, faithful and essentially surjective. We construct G, ε and η in turn.

Objects. For each object Y of 𝒟, essential surjectivity provides some object of 𝒞 whose image is isomorphic to Y. Choose one, call it G Y, and choose an isomorphism εY : F(G Y) → Y.

Arrows. Given k : Y → Y′, the composite εY′−1 ∘ k ∘ εY is an arrow from F(G Y) to F(G Y′). Because F is full and faithful, it is F of exactly one arrow G Y → G Y′. Call that arrow G k. So G k is defined by the equation

F(G k) = εY′−1 ∘ k ∘ εY.

G is a functor. F(G(idY)) = εY−1 ∘ εY = id = F(idG Y), so G(idY) = idG Y because F is faithful. For composites, F(G k′ ∘ G k) = F(G k′) ∘ F(G k), and substituting the defining equation twice the inner ε and ε−1 cancel, leaving ε−1 ∘ (k′ ∘ k) ∘ ε = F(G(k′ ∘ k)). Faithfulness gives G k′ ∘ G k = G(k′ ∘ k).

ε is a natural isomorphism. Multiply the defining equation on the left by εY′: it becomes εY′ ∘ F G k = k ∘ εY, which is precisely the naturality square of ε : F ∘ G ⇒ Id. Its components are isomorphisms by choice.

η. For an object X of 𝒞, the arrow εF X−1 : F X → F G F X is an isomorphism between objects in the image of F. By fullness and faithfulness it is F of a unique arrow ηX : X → G F X, and ηX is an isomorphism because fully faithful functors reflect isomorphisms (Lesson 2, §6). For naturality we must show G F f ∘ ηX = ηX′ ∘ f; as F is faithful it is enough to check this after applying F, where it reads

F G F f ∘ εF X−1 = εF X′−1 ∘ F f.

That is the naturality square of ε at the arrow F f, rearranged. ∎

Aside · Where the choices went

The second half began with the word choose, once for every object of 𝒟. The functor G therefore depends on those choices — a different choice of objects G Y and isomorphisms εY gives a different pseudo-inverse. But any two pseudo-inverses of F are naturally isomorphic (Exercise 2), so nothing that category theory can express depends on the choice. In practice the theorem means one almost never writes G down: exhibit F, check three properties, and stop.

§5 — A gallery of equivalences

The theorem at work

With the recognition theorem in hand, the three examples of §1 are settled in a few lines each, and several more come within reach.

i. Finite sets

The inclusion Fin → FinSet sends each standard set [n] to itself and each function to itself. It is full and faithful, because the functions between [m] and [n] are the same in both categories. It is essentially surjective, because a finite set with n elements is in bijection with [n], and bijections are the isomorphisms of FinSet. Hence Fin ≃ FinSet. Notice that we never had to choose a numbering of every finite set — that choice is the pseudo-inverse, and the theorem makes it unnecessary.

ii. Matrices and linear maps

Define F : Mat → FinVect by sending the object n to the space ℝn and an m × n matrix A to the linear map x ↦ A x. It is a functor, since the matrix product BA corresponds to the composite of the linear maps. It is faithful, since different matrices give different maps, and full, since every linear map ℝn → ℝm is multiplication by a matrix. And it is essentially surjective, since every finite-dimensional space has a basis and so is isomorphic to some ℝn. Therefore

Mat ≃ FinVect.

Here the pseudo-inverse is “pick a basis for every vector space, and write every linear map as a matrix in those bases” — a wildly non-canonical procedure. That the two categories are nevertheless equivalent is the precise sense in which the choice of bases does not matter.

iii. A preorder and its poset

In a preorder P, declare x ∼ y when x ≤ y and y ≤ x — when x and y are isomorphic, in the language of Lesson 1. The classes of this equivalence relation form a partial order P/∼, and the quotient map P → P/∼ is a functor: monotone, hence faithful; full, because [x] ≤ [y] means exactly x ≤ y; and surjective on objects. So every preorder is equivalent to a partial order. For the integers under divisibility, n ∼ −n, and the quotient is the natural numbers under divisibility: the signs were never doing anything.

iv. Partial functions and pointed sets

Let Par be the category of sets and partial functions — functions that may be undefined at some inputs — and let Set∗ be the category of pointed sets: sets with a chosen base point, and functions that send base point to base point. Define F : Par → Set∗ by adding a new point: F X = X ∪ {⊥}, with base point ⊥, and for a partial function f let F f send x to f(x) where that is defined, and to ⊥ where it is not (and ⊥ to ⊥). The new point is a value meaning “undefined.” This functor is full and faithful — a base-point-preserving function X ∪ {⊥} → Y ∪ {⊥} is the same information as a partial function X → Y, undefined exactly where the value is ⊥ — and essentially surjective, since any pointed set is isomorphic to its non-base points with a new base point added. So Par ≃ Set∗. Programmers know this equivalence as the idea of representing failure by a special value; it returns in Lesson 10 as the Maybe monad.

v. A category of bijections and a group

Take the category whose objects are all three-element sets and whose arrows are the bijections between them. Inside it sits the one-object full subcategory on the set {1, 2, 3}, whose arrows are the six permutations: the symmetric group S3, viewed as a one-object category. The inclusion is full and faithful by definition, and essentially surjective because every three-element set is in bijection with {1, 2, 3}. So the huge category of all three-element sets and bijections is equivalent to a single group with six elements. The pattern 𝟙 ≃ ℐ of §2 is the same phenomenon in miniature.

§6 — What equivalence preserves

Categorical properties, and skeletons

If equivalent categories are “the same,” then every property worth stating should pass from one to the other. The properties that do are called categorical. This section tests the claim on the properties we have met, and identifies the ones that fail — which turn out to be exactly the ones Lesson 1 told us not to care about.

Proposition · An equivalence preserves and reflects everything so far

Let F : 𝒞 → 𝒟 be an equivalence. Then:

  • X ≅ X′ in 𝒞 if and only if F X ≅ F X′ in 𝒟;
  • the hom-sets 𝒞(X, X′) and 𝒟(F X, F X′) are in bijection;
  • an arrow f is an isomorphism, a monomorphism, or an epimorphism if and only if F f is.

Proof. The second statement is full faithfulness. The first, and the statement about isomorphisms, are Lesson 2’s theorems that functors preserve isomorphisms and fully faithful functors reflect them. Faithful functors reflect monos and epis (Lesson 2, §6), so what remains is that F preserves them — which is not true of functors in general, and uses all three properties at once.

Let m : X → X′ be monic, and suppose F m ∘ h = F m ∘ k for some arrows h, k : Y → F X in 𝒟. The object Y need not be in the image of F, but by essential surjectivity there is an isomorphism e : F W → Y for some W. Then h ∘ e and k ∘ e are arrows F W → F X, so by fullness h ∘ e = F h0 and k ∘ e = F k0 for some h0, k0 : W → X. Now

F(m ∘ h0) = F m ∘ h ∘ e = F m ∘ k ∘ e = F(m ∘ k0),

so m ∘ h0 = m ∘ k0 by faithfulness, hence h0 = k0 because m is monic, hence h ∘ e = k ∘ e, hence h = k because e is invertible. So F m is monic. The argument for epimorphisms is the mirror image. ∎

The same pattern — move the problem into the image of F using essential surjectivity, pull it back with fullness, solve it in 𝒞, return with faithfulness — transfers every construction in the rest of this course: terminal objects, products, limits, adjoints. You may take it as a standing fact that all of them are preserved by equivalences.

What is not preserved? The number of objects: 𝟙 has one and ℐ has two. Whether a category has “exactly one object in each isomorphism class.” Whether two objects are equal. These are not defects of the notion of equivalence. They are properties that depend on how a category happens to be presented, not on its structure, and equivalence is designed to ignore them.

Skeletons

There is a vivid way to say what equivalence ignores. Call a category skeletal if isomorphic objects are always equal — no object is present in two copies. A skeleton of a category 𝒞 is a full subcategory that contains exactly one object from each isomorphism class of 𝒞.

Theorem · Skeletons

Every category is equivalent to each of its skeletons. And an equivalence between two skeletal categories is an isomorphism of categories.

Proof. The inclusion of a skeleton is full and faithful, like the inclusion of any full subcategory, and it is essentially surjective because every object is isomorphic to the chosen representative of its class. That proves the first statement.

For the second, let F : 𝒞 → 𝒟 be an equivalence between skeletal categories. Every object Y of 𝒟 is isomorphic to some F X, hence equal to it: F is surjective on objects. If F X = F X′ then X ≅ X′ by the proposition above, hence X = X′: F is injective on objects. A functor that is bijective on objects and bijective on each hom-set has an inverse functor. ∎

So two categories are equivalent exactly when their skeletons are isomorphic. Equivalence is isomorphism after the redundant copies of objects have been thrown away. The standard finite sets Fin form a skeleton of FinSet; the spaces ℝn form a skeleton of FinVect, isomorphic to Mat; a partial order is the skeleton of a preorder; and 𝟙 is the skeleton of ℐ.

§7 — Equivalences by hand

Finite categories, compared on paper

For finite categories everything in this lesson can be carried out with pencil and paper. Three worked examples: verifying an equivalence completely, finding a skeleton, and proving that two categories are not equivalent.

The equivalence of 𝟙 and ℐ, in full

Recall the data from §2: F picks the object a; G collapses everything; η is the identity; εa = ida and εb = u. The composite F ∘ G sends every arrow of ℐ to ida. The category ℐ has four arrows, so there are four naturality squares for ε, each of the form k ∘ εY = εY′ ∘ ida for an arrow k : Y → Y′:

arrow kk ∘ ε at its sourceε at its targetequal?
ida : a → aida ∘ ida = idaidayes
idb : b → bidb ∘ u = uuyes
u : a → bu ∘ ida = uuyes
u−1 : b → au−1 ∘ u = idaidayes

All four squares commute and both components are isomorphisms. The last row is the only one that uses the equation u−1 ∘ u = ida: it is exactly the fact that u is an isomorphism that makes the second object redundant. Remove u−1 from the category — leaving 𝟚 — and the component εb = u is no longer invertible; indeed 𝟚 is not equivalent to 𝟙, as the third example below shows.

Finding a skeleton

Order the eight numbers ±1, ±2, ±3, ±6 by divisibility. This is a preorder with n ≅ −n for each n, so there are four isomorphism classes, and choosing the positive member of each gives the skeleton {1, 2, 3, 6} — the divisibility diamond that will accompany us through Lessons 6 to 9.

The count of arrows shows how much was redundant. The diamond has 9 arrows (count the pairs with a | b among 1, 2, 3, 6). In the eight-object preorder each of those becomes 2 × 2 = 4 arrows, for 36 in all. Thirty-six arrows and eight objects on one side, nine arrows and four objects on the other: equivalent categories.

Proving two categories are not equivalent

To show 𝒞 ≄ 𝒟 it is enough to find one categorical property they do not share. The cheapest are counts that survive passing to a skeleton: the number of isomorphism classes of objects, and the sizes of hom-sets between them. Compare four small categories, each with one or two objects:

CategoryIsomorphism classesHom-sets between the classes
𝟙1one arrow from the class to itself
ℐ1one arrow from the class to itself
𝟚2one arrow in one direction, none in the other
two objects, only identities2no arrows between the classes

The first two are equivalent, as we know. Neither is equivalent to the last two, which have two classes. And the last two are not equivalent to each other: an equivalence would give a bijection between hom-sets, but 𝟚 has a hom-set of size one between its two classes and the discrete category has none. One more consequence of the skeleton theorem deserves a line: a group, viewed as a one-object category, is skeletal, so two groups are equivalent as categories only if they are isomorphic as groups.

Optional · PythonSkeletons and the three-part test, by machine

For finite categories both sides of the recognition theorem are computable. The setup block is Lesson 1’s category checker and Lesson 2’s functor checker, carried over so that this page runs on its own.

setup.py — carried over from Lessons 1 and 2
from itertools import product

class FiniteCategory:
    """objects, arrows (name -> (dom, cod)), a composition table,
       and a chosen identity arrow for each object."""
    def __init__(self, objects, arrows, comp, ident):
        self.obj, self.ar = set(objects), arrows
        self.comp, self.id = comp, ident

    def dom(self, a): return self.ar[a][0]
    def cod(self, a): return self.ar[a][1]
    def composable(self, g, f): return self.dom(g) == self.cod(f)

    def check(self):
        # 1. each identity is typed X -> X
        for X in self.obj:
            assert self.ar[self.id[X]] == (X, X)
        # 2. every composable pair has a composite, correctly typed
        for g, f in product(self.ar, repeat=2):
            if self.composable(g, f):
                gf = self.comp[(g, f)]
                assert self.ar[gf] == (self.dom(f), self.cod(g))
        # 3. identity law on both sides
        for f in self.ar:
            X, Y = self.ar[f]
            assert self.comp[(self.id[Y], f)] == f
            assert self.comp[(f, self.id[X])] == f
        # 4. associativity
        for h, g, f in product(self.ar, repeat=3):
            if self.composable(h, g) and self.composable(g, f):
                assert self.comp[(h, self.comp[(g, f)])] == \
                       self.comp[(self.comp[(h, g)], f)]
        return True

class Functor:
    """F : C -> D, given by a map on objects and a map on arrows."""
    def __init__(self, C, D, on_obj, on_arr):
        self.C, self.D, self.on_obj, self.on_arr = C, D, on_obj, on_arr

    def check(self):
        C, D, Fo, Fa = self.C, self.D, self.on_obj, self.on_arr
        for f in C.ar:                        # typing: F f : F X -> F Y
            X, Y = C.ar[f]
            assert D.ar[Fa[f]] == (Fo[X], Fo[Y])
        for X in C.obj:                       # preserves identities
            assert Fa[C.id[X]] == D.id[Fo[X]]
        for g, f in product(C.ar, repeat=2):  # preserves composition
            if C.composable(g, f):
                assert Fa[C.comp[(g, f)]] == D.comp[(Fa[g], Fa[f])]
        return True

def arrows_between(C, X, Y):
    "all arrows X -> Y of the finite category C"
    return [a for a, (d, c) in C.ar.items() if d == X and c == Y]

def make_poset(objs, leq):
    "a finite preorder as a thin category; the arrow a -> b is named 'a<=b'"
    ar = {f"{a}<={b}": (a, b) for a in objs for b in objs if leq(a, b)}
    comp = {(g, f): f"{ar[f][0]}<={ar[g][1]}"
            for g in ar for f in ar if ar[f][1] == ar[g][0]}
    return FiniteCategory(objs, ar, comp, {a: f"{a}<={a}" for a in objs})

First, isomorphism classes and a skeleton. An arrow is an isomorphism when some arrow composes with it to an identity on both sides; two objects are in the same class when an isomorphism joins them.

skeleton.py — isomorphism classes and a skeleton
def is_iso(C, f):
    X, Y = C.ar[f]
    return any(C.comp[(g, f)] == C.id[X] and C.comp[(f, g)] == C.id[Y]
               for g in arrows_between(C, Y, X))

def iso_classes(C):
    classes = []
    for X in sorted(C.obj, key=str):
        for cl in classes:
            if any(is_iso(C, f) for f in arrows_between(C, X, cl[0])):
                cl.append(X); break
        else:
            classes.append([X])
    return classes

def skeleton(C):
    "the full subcategory on one object from each isomorphism class"
    keep = {max(cl) for cl in iso_classes(C)}      # one object per class
    ar = {a: dc for a, dc in C.ar.items()
          if dc[0] in keep and dc[1] in keep}
    comp = {(g, f): C.comp[(g, f)]
            for g in ar for f in ar if ar[f][1] == ar[g][0]}
    return FiniteCategory(keep, ar, comp, {X: C.id[X] for X in keep})

divides = lambda a, b: b % a == 0
Signed = make_poset({1, -1, 2, -2, 3, -3, 6, -6}, divides)
Sk = skeleton(Signed)
print("classes :", iso_classes(Signed))
print("arrows  :", len(Signed.ar), "in the preorder,",
      len(Sk.ar), "in its skeleton", sorted(Sk.obj))
print("skeleton is a category:", Sk.check())

Next, the three properties, each a direct transcription of its definition, and the recognition theorem as a one-line test.

three_part_test.py — faithful, full, essentially surjective
def faithful(F):
    C = F.C
    return all(F.on_arr[f] != F.on_arr[g]
               for f in C.ar for g in C.ar
               if f != g and C.ar[f] == C.ar[g])

def full(F):
    C, D = F.C, F.D
    return all({F.on_arr[f] for f in arrows_between(C, X, Y)}
               == set(arrows_between(D, F.on_obj[X], F.on_obj[Y]))
               for X in C.obj for Y in C.obj)

def essentially_surjective(F):
    D = F.D
    reached = lambda Y: any(is_iso(D, k) for X in F.C.obj
                            for k in arrows_between(D, F.on_obj[X], Y))
    return all(reached(Y) for Y in D.obj)

def is_equivalence(F):
    return faithful(F) and full(F) and essentially_surjective(F)

Going deeper

Dualities: equivalences with an opposite category

Some of the most celebrated theorems in mathematics are equivalences of a special kind: between one category and the opposite of another. Such an equivalence is called a duality. It says that two subjects are mirror images — every construction in one is a construction in the other with all arrows reversed.

The first example has been in front of us since Lesson 3. Sending a finite-dimensional vector space to its dual, V ↦ V∗, is a contravariant functor, that is, a functor D : FinVectop → FinVect. Applying it twice gives the double dual, and Lesson 3 proved that the evaluation maps evV : V → V∗∗ form a natural isomorphism. That natural isomorphism is exactly what is needed to make D an equivalence:

FinVectop ≃ FinVect.

Finite-dimensional linear algebra is its own mirror image: for every theorem about subspaces there is a twin about quotients, for every statement about injective linear maps a twin about surjective ones.

Other dualities connect subjects that look unrelated. The category of finite sets is dual to the category of finite Boolean algebras: a finite set X corresponds to the algebra of its subsets, and a function f to the inverse-image map f−1 running the other way — the contravariant powerset functor of Lesson 2, promoted to an equivalence. Its far-reaching generalisations (Stone duality for Boolean algebras and certain topological spaces, Gelfand duality for spaces and algebras of functions on them) are the source of a slogan worth remembering: algebra is dual to geometry. Each time, the content of the theorem is that a functor to an opposite category is full, faithful and essentially surjective.

Not every category is its own mirror image, however. Lesson 6 gives a one-line reason why Set is not equivalent to Setop: the two ends of Set — the empty set and the one-point set — behave differently.

§8 — Exercises

Fourteen problems on equivalence

The proofs in this lesson have a characteristic move — transfer a question along an isomorphism, solve it, transfer back — and the arrows-only exercises are practice at making it. The problems are grouped by topic, following the lesson’s sections, and within each group they run from easier to harder. Exercise 10 has an optional Python part; everything else is pencil and paper.

The definition

§2–§3 · Exercises 1–2

Exercise 1by hand

In §2 the functor F : 𝟙 → ℐ picked out the object a. Repeat the construction with the functor F′ that picks out b instead: write down the components of a natural isomorphism ε′ : F′ ∘ G ⇒ Id and check the naturality square at the arrow u : a → b.

Show solution

Now F′ ∘ G sends both objects to b and every arrow to idb. The components must be isomorphisms ε′a : b → a and ε′b : b → b, and the only candidates are ε′a = u−1 and ε′b = idb. The square at u asks that u ∘ ε′a = ε′b ∘ idb, that is, u ∘ u−1 = idb. True.

Both F and F′ are equivalences, with the same pseudo-inverse G. A pseudo-inverse determines a functor only up to natural isomorphism — here F ≅ F′, by the natural isomorphism whose single component is u.

Exercise 2arrows-only

Pseudo-inverses are unique up to natural isomorphism. Let (F, G, η, ε) and (F, G′, η′, ε′) be two equivalences with the same functor F : 𝒞 → 𝒟. Show that G ≅ G′ by considering, for each object Y of 𝒟, the composite

G Y → G′ F G Y → G′ Y,

whose first arrow is η′G Y and whose second is G′(εY).

Show solution

Both arrows are isomorphisms: η′G Y is a component of the natural isomorphism η′, and G′(εY) is the image under a functor of the isomorphism εY. So each composite θY = G′(εY) ∘ η′G Y is an isomorphism G Y → G′ Y.

For naturality, observe that the first arrows are the components of η′ whiskered by G on the right — the transformation η′G : G ⇒ G′ F G — and the second arrows are the components of ε whiskered by G′ on the left, G′ε : G′ F G ⇒ G′. Both whiskerings are natural (Lesson 3, Going deeper), and a composite of natural transformations is natural. So θ : G ⇒ G′ is a natural transformation whose components are isomorphisms: a natural isomorphism.

This is why the choices made in the proof of the recognition theorem are harmless.

The recognition theorem

§4 · Exercises 3–6

Exercise 3warm-up

Let F : 𝒞 → 𝒟 be an isomorphism of categories, with inverse G.

  1. Exhibit F as an equivalence: what are η and ε?
  2. Verify directly, without the recognition theorem, that F is full, faithful and essentially surjective.
Show solution

(a) Since G ∘ F = Id and F ∘ G = Id exactly, take η and ε to be the identity natural transformations, whose components are identity arrows. They are natural isomorphisms (Lesson 3, Exercise 11).

(b) Faithful: if F f = F f′ then f = G F f = G F f′ = f′. Full: an arrow k : F X → F X′ equals F(G k), and G k runs from G F X = X to X′. Essentially surjective: every Y is actually equal to F(G Y) — more than was asked. The difference between an isomorphism and an equivalence of categories is exactly the difference between “equal to” and “isomorphic to” in this last clause.

Exercise 4examples

For each functor, decide whether it is an equivalence. Where it is not, say which of the three properties fails.

  1. The inclusion of the even integers into the integers, both ordered by ≤.
  2. The only functor from the discrete category on two objects to 𝟙.
  3. The inclusion into FinSet of its full subcategory of non-empty finite sets.
  4. The functor from the integers under divisibility to the natural numbers under divisibility that sends n to |n|.
Show solution

(a) No. It is full and faithful, but an odd number is not isomorphic to any even one — in a poset, isomorphic objects are equal — so it is not essentially surjective.

(b) No. It is faithful and essentially surjective, but not full: there is no arrow between the two objects of the discrete category to map onto the identity of 𝟙.

(c) No. Full and faithful, as for any full subcategory; but the empty set is not in bijection with any non-empty set, so essential surjectivity fails.

(d) Yes. Functors between preorders are faithful. It is full: if |m| divides |n| then m divides n. It is surjective on objects. This is the quotient of a preorder onto its poset, example (iii) of §5.

Exercise 5arrows-only

Let (F, G, η, ε) be an equivalence between 𝒞 and 𝒟. Show that for all objects X of 𝒞 and Y of 𝒟, the function

𝒟(F X, Y) → 𝒞(X, G Y),     k ↦ G k ∘ ηX

is a bijection.

Show solution

The function is a composite of two bijections. First, k ↦ G k is a bijection from 𝒟(F X, Y) to 𝒞(G F X, G Y), because G is an equivalence and therefore full and faithful. Second, h ↦ h ∘ ηX is a bijection from 𝒞(G F X, G Y) to 𝒞(X, G Y), with inverse h′ ↦ h′ ∘ ηX−1, because ηX is an isomorphism (Lesson 1, §5).

So arrows out of F X in one category correspond exactly to arrows into G Y in the other. Remember the shape of this bijection: a pair of functors related in this way, even when they are far from being an equivalence, is called an adjunction, and it is the subject of Lesson 8.

Exercise 6think

Let G and H be groups, viewed as one-object categories. Show that G and H are equivalent as categories if and only if they are isomorphic as groups.

Show solution

A group viewed as a category has a single object, so it is certainly skeletal. By the skeleton theorem of §6, an equivalence between skeletal categories is an isomorphism of categories. And an isomorphism between one-object categories is a bijection on arrows preserving composition and identity — a group isomorphism. The converse is Exercise 3.

It is instructive to see the direct argument too. An equivalence F is full and faithful, so on the single hom-set it is a bijection from the elements of G to the elements of H; being a functor, it preserves the operation. For one-object categories, “equivalent” has no extra room: all the slack in the notion of equivalence comes from having several isomorphic objects, and here there is only one object.

§5 · Exercises 7–8

Exercise 7examples

Check the equivalence Par ≃ Set∗ of §5 on a small case. Let X = {1, 2} and Y = {a}.

  1. List all partial functions from X to Y.
  2. List all base-point-preserving functions from X ∪ {⊥} to Y ∪ {⊥}, and match them with your answers to (a).
  3. The pointed set ({0, 1, 2}, 0) is not literally of the form X ∪ {⊥} with ⊥ a new point. Why does this not prevent the functor from being an equivalence?
Show solution

(a) Each of 1 and 2 is either sent to a or left undefined: four partial functions — defined on both, on 1 only, on 2 only, or nowhere.

(b) ⊥ must go to ⊥; each of 1 and 2 may go to a or to ⊥: four functions. The match sends a partial function to the function that returns ⊥ exactly where the partial function is undefined. So the hom-set map is a bijection, as full faithfulness requires.

(c) Essential surjectivity asks only that every pointed set be isomorphic to one in the image. And ({0, 1, 2}, 0) is isomorphic to {1, 2} ∪ {⊥} by the bijection sending 0 to ⊥ and fixing 1 and 2, which respects base points. The functor is not surjective on objects, and Par and Set∗ are not isomorphic categories in any evident way — but they are equivalent, and that is the notion that matters.

Skeletons

§6–§7 · Exercises 9–12

Exercise 9counting

Let 𝒞 be the category whose objects are the four subsets of {a, b} and whose arrows are all functions between them.

  1. Find a skeleton of 𝒞.
  2. Count the arrows of 𝒞 and the arrows of the skeleton. (The number of functions from a set with m elements to a set with n elements is nm, with 00 = 1.)
Show solution

(a) Two finite sets are isomorphic exactly when they have the same number of elements. The sizes of the four objects ∅, {a}, {b}, {a, b} are 0, 1, 1, 2, so there are three classes, and ∅, {a}, {a, b} is a skeleton.

(b) Add up nm over all ordered pairs. From ∅ there is exactly one function to each object: 4 arrows in 𝒞. From a one-element set there are 0 + 1 + 1 + 2 = 4, and there are two such sets: 8. From {a, b} there are 0 + 1 + 1 + 4 = 6. Total: 18. In the skeleton: from ∅, 3; from {a}, 0 + 1 + 2 = 3; from {a, b}, 0 + 1 + 4 = 5. Total: 11.

Eighteen arrows against eleven, four objects against three — and the two categories are equivalent.

Exercise 11structure

Let P be the set of all non-zero integers, ordered by divisibility.

  1. Which pairs of elements are isomorphic? Describe the isomorphism classes.
  2. Describe a skeleton of P, and a functor from P onto it that is an equivalence.
  3. In P, how many arrows are there from the class of 2 to the class of 12, counting all choices of representatives? How many in the skeleton?
Show solution

(a) m ≅ n means each divides the other, which happens exactly when m = ±n. The classes are the pairs {n, −n}.

(b) The positive integers under divisibility form a skeleton: one object from each class, with the same order. The functor n ↦ |n| is an equivalence by Exercise 4(d).

(c) There is an arrow m → n whenever m ∈ {2, −2} and n ∈ {12, −12}, since each of ±2 divides each of ±12: four arrows. In the skeleton there is one, 2 → 12. An equivalence does not preserve the number of arrows in total, but it does preserve the size of each individual hom-set — here, one.

Exercise 12think

Let ℬ be the category of all finite sets and all bijections between them.

  1. Describe a skeleton of ℬ.
  2. Explain the slogan: “ℬ is equivalent to the symmetric groups S0, S1, S2, … placed side by side.”
Show solution

(a) As in FinSet, the standard sets [0], [1], [2], … contain exactly one object from each isomorphism class. So the skeleton is the full subcategory on these objects.

(b) In the skeleton, the arrows from [n] to itself are the bijections of an n-element set, the permutation group Sn. And there are no arrows at all from [m] to [n] when m ≠ n, since no bijection exists. So the skeleton is a collection of one-object categories with no arrows between them: the groups Sn, one for each n, sitting side by side. A category in which every arrow is invertible is a groupoid, and this exercise is the general picture of one: up to equivalence, a groupoid is just a family of groups.

What equivalence preserves

§6, Going deeper · Exercises 13–14

Exercise 13arrows-only

Let F : 𝒞 → 𝒟 be an equivalence. Prove that F preserves epimorphisms, by adapting the proof in §6 that it preserves monomorphisms. Say exactly where each of the three properties of F is used.

Show solution

Let e : X → X′ be epic, and suppose h ∘ F e = k ∘ F e for arrows h, k : F X′ → Y. By essential surjectivity there is an isomorphism i : Y → F W for some object W of 𝒞. The arrows i ∘ h and i ∘ k run F X′ → F W, so by fullness they are F h0 and F k0 for some h0, k0 : X′ → W. Then

F(h0 ∘ e) = i ∘ h ∘ F e = i ∘ k ∘ F e = F(k0 ∘ e),

so h0 ∘ e = k0 ∘ e by faithfulness, and h0 = k0 because e is epic. Hence i ∘ h = i ∘ k, and cancelling the isomorphism i gives h = k.

The only change from the monomorphism proof is the side on which the isomorphism is attached: the test object Y now sits at the target, so we transport it into the image of F by composing on the left.

Exercise 14structure

The dual vector space functor gives an equivalence FinVectop ≃ FinVect (Going deeper). Use it, together with §6, to explain each of the following without any computation.

  1. A linear map T between finite-dimensional spaces is injective if and only if its dual T∗ is surjective.
  2. T is an isomorphism if and only if T∗ is.

You may use that in FinVect the monomorphisms are the injective linear maps and the epimorphisms are the surjective ones.

Show solution

Write D for the dual functor FinVectop → FinVect. An arrow of FinVectop is an arrow of FinVect with source and target exchanged, and reversing arrows exchanges the two cancellation properties: T is monic in FinVect exactly when it is epic in FinVectop.

(a) T is injective ⟺ T is monic in FinVect ⟺ T is epic in FinVectop ⟺ D(T) = T∗ is epic in FinVect (an equivalence preserves and reflects epimorphisms) ⟺ T∗ is surjective.

(b) Isomorphisms are the same in a category and its opposite, and an equivalence preserves and reflects them.

Dualities turn every theorem into two. That is the practical value of knowing that a contravariant functor is an equivalence.

Recap

What to carry forward

  1. Isomorphism of categories demands G F X = X — an equality of objects — and is too strict to be useful: finite sets, linear algebra, and even the two-object category ℐ fail it for no good reason.
  2. An equivalence asks instead for natural isomorphisms Id ≅ G ∘ F and F ∘ G ≅ Id. Equivalence is reflexive, symmetric and transitive.
  3. Recognition theorem: a functor is an equivalence exactly when it is full, faithful and essentially surjective. The pseudo-inverse is built by choices, and is unique up to natural isomorphism.
  4. Equivalences preserve and reflect every categorical property — isomorphism of objects, sizes of hom-sets, monos, epis — and ignore only how many copies of each object are present.
  5. Every category is equivalent to its skeleton; two categories are equivalent exactly when their skeletons are isomorphic. A duality is an equivalence with an opposite category.
