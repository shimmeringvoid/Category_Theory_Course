§1 — What does “free” mean?

A word we have owed a definition since Lesson 2

Lesson 2 met the free monoid functor Set → Mon and remarked that free and forgetful functors travel in matched pairs. It is time to pay that debt. Write U : Mon → Set for the forgetful functor, which sends a monoid to its underlying set. The free monoid on a set X is the set of finite lists over X, with concatenation as the operation and the empty list as the unit — but “lists with concatenation” is an implementation. What is the interface? What property makes this monoid, and no other, the free one?

Here it is. To give a monoid homomorphism out of List X into any monoid M, it is enough — and necessary — to say where the generators go: any function f : X → U M extends to exactly one homomorphism f̂ : List X → M, namely f̂([x₁, …, xₙ]) = f(x₁) · ⋯ · f(xₙ). Programmers know this operation as foldMap. “Free” means: no relations beyond the forced ones, so homomorphisms out are as easy as functions in. (Lesson 5 said the same thing in other words: the lists, with the function x ↦ [x], are the initial object of the comma category (X ↓ U). §5 returns to that description.)

Squint at the phrase “exactly one” and you will recognize a universal property; but this one has a new shape. It is not about arrows into a single object — it is a correspondence between two different kinds of arrow, living in two different categories:

Mon(List X, M)  ≅  Set(X, U M).

Homomorphisms from the free thing on one side; plain functions to the underlying set on the other; a perfect bijection between them. Abstract the pattern — replace List and U by any pair of opposite-pointing functors — and you have the definition of an adjunction. This lesson is a tour of how much of mathematics turns out to have exactly this shape.

§2 — The definition

Arrows traded at par

Definition · Adjunction

Let F : 𝒞 → 𝒟 and U : 𝒟 → 𝒞 be functors. An adjunction F ⊣ U (“F is left adjoint to U”) is a bijection

𝒟(F X, Y)  ≅  𝒞(X, U Y)

for every X in 𝒞 and Y in 𝒟, natural in both. The matched arrows f̂ : F X → Y and f : X → U Y are called each other’s transposes.

The mnemonic for the direction: the left adjoint appears on the left of the hom-set. And “natural in both” is not fine print — it is what makes the bijection a single coherent conversion rather than a family of coincidences. Concretely it says transposition commutes with composition on both flanks: for any h : X′ → X in 𝒞 and k : Y → Y′ in 𝒟, the transpose of k ∘ f̂ ∘ F h is U k ∘ f ∘ h. Pre-process the input or post-process the output, then transpose — or transpose first and do the same on the other side; you land on the same arrow. (This is the naturality of Lesson 3, applied in both variables at once; it is spelled out as two equations at the end of this section.)

In the free-monoid case the bijection is exactly foldMap one way and “restrict to generators” the other. And the definition makes precise Lesson 2’s remark that free and forgetful functors travel in matched pairs. They are two halves of one mechanism — U throws away structure, F adds the least possible structure, and the adjunction is the precise sense in which those are inverse tendencies without being inverse functors.

Naturality, spelled out

Everything later in the lesson leans on the words “natural in both,” so here they are as two equations. Write φ for the bijection from 𝒟(F X, Y) to 𝒞(X, U Y), so that φ(f̂) = f. Then

φ(k ∘ f̂) = U k ∘ φ(f̂)     for every k : Y → Y′      (natural in Y),

φ(f̂ ∘ F h) = φ(f̂) ∘ h     for every h : X′ → X      (natural in X).

Each is a naturality square in the sense of Lesson 3. For a fixed X, the first says that the bijections φ form a natural isomorphism between two functors 𝒟 → Set, namely 𝒟(F X, −) and 𝒞(X, U −). For a fixed Y, the second says the same of two contravariant functors of X.

For lists, both equations are familiar facts. Let f : X → U M be a function into a monoid, with transpose f̂, the homomorphism that sends a list [x1, …, xn] to the product f(x1) · ⋯ · f(xn).

  • Natural in Y. If k : M → M′ is a homomorphism, then k ∘ f̂ sends the list to k(f(x1) · ⋯ · f(xn)), while the transpose of U k ∘ f sends it to k(f(x1)) · ⋯ · k(f(xn)). These agree — precisely because k preserves products and the unit.
  • Natural in X. If h : X′ → X is a function, then f̂ ∘ List(h) first relabels each letter of a list by h and then multiplies out; the transpose of f ∘ h does both at once.

Without naturality a bijection of hom-sets would say very little: any two sets of the same size are in bijection, in many unrelated ways. Naturality ties the bijections for different X and Y to one another, and all the consequences drawn in §4, §5 and §6 come from that.

Currying

Fix a set A. A function of two arguments X × A → Y and a function-valued function X → YA carry the same information — curry and uncurry convert between them, and the conversion respects pre- and post-composition. That is an adjunction (−) × A ⊣ (−)A, and it is the universal property that defines function types: YA is the object that makes the bijection Set(X × A, Y) ≅ Set(X, YA) work. Galois connections: adjunctions between posets

Run the definition in thin categories. Functors between posets are monotone maps (Lesson 2), each hom-set has at most one element, so “bijection of hom-sets” collapses to a two-way implication: monotone f and g form an adjunction f ⊣ g exactly when

f(x) ≤ y  ⟺  x ≤ g(y)   for all x, y.

Order theorists call this a Galois connection. The everyday example: include the integers into the reals, i : ℤ ↪ ℝ. Then ⌈r⌉ ≤ n ⟺ r ≤ n makes ceiling a left adjoint of the inclusion, and n ≤ ⌊r⌋ ⟺ n ≤ r makes floor a right adjoint: ⌈−⌉ ⊣ i ⊣ ⌊−⌋. Rounding up is the best over-approximation, rounding down the best under-approximation — left adjoints approach from one side, right adjoints from the other, and the inclusion sits between its own two corrections. §7 computes adjoints of this kind by hand, and its optional lab verifies both equivalences on 1,586 instances and builds a finder for the adjoints of arbitrary monotone maps between finite posets.

The diagonal, flanked

Lesson 6’s constructions join the gallery too. The diagonal functor Δ : 𝒞 → 𝒞 × 𝒞 sends X to the pair (X, X); a right adjoint to it must convert arrows (X, X) → (A, B) — i.e. pairs of arrows — into single arrows X → ?, and Lesson 6’s product definition says the product does exactly that. Dually the coproduct is Δ’s left adjoint: + ⊣ Δ ⊣ ×, another sandwich (Exercise 9). This generalizes: replacing Δ by the constant-diagram functor of Lesson 7, limits are right adjoints and colimits are left adjoints — the previous two lessons were secretly case studies of this one.

§4 — Unit, counit, and consequences

The adjunction, repackaged as two natural transformations

The bijection can be distilled into two canonical arrows. Transpose the identity idF X : F X → F X and get the unit ηX : X → U F X; transpose idU Y the other way and get the counit εY : F U Y → Y. Naturality of the bijection makes η : Id ⇒ U∘F and ε : F∘U ⇒ Id natural transformations — Lesson 3’s machinery, in a starring role. In the free-monoid adjunction, η is the singleton map x ↦ [x] (“a generator, regarded as a one-term word”) and ε evaluates a formal word of monoid elements down to an actual product. In currying, ε is eval.

Definition · Adjunction, take two

An adjunction F ⊣ U may equivalently be given by natural transformations η : Id ⇒ U∘F and ε : F∘U ⇒ Id satisfying the two triangle identities:

εF X ∘ F(ηX) = idF X   and   U(εY) ∘ ηU Y = idU Y.

Each direction of the equivalence is a construction. From a bijection: naturality in Y, applied to the identity of F X, shows that the transpose of any f̂ : F X → Y is U f̂ ∘ ηX — so the unit alone determines the whole bijection — and dually the transpose of f : X → U Y is εY ∘ F f. The triangle identities then say that ηX and εY, transposed back, are the identities they came from. From η and ε: define transposition by those same two formulas, and the triangle identities are exactly what force the two to be mutually inverse. Exercise 12 proves that — it is the best composition workout the course has to offer — and Exercise 13 supplies the remaining details in both directions. In the free-monoid case the triangles say something you can whisper in plain language: “wrap each generator as a word, then evaluate: you get the word back” and “wrap a monoid element, then evaluate: you get the element back.”

Theorem · RAPL

Right adjoints preserve limits; left adjoints preserve colimits. If U has a left adjoint, then U(lim D) ≅ lim (U∘D) for every diagram D with a limit — and dually for F and colimits.

§6 proves the theorem, twice. You have known an instance of it for years: the underlying set of a product of groups is the product of the underlying sets. Now you know why — U : Grp → Set has a left adjoint (free groups), so it must preserve products, terminal objects, equalizers, pullbacks, all of Lesson 7’s zoo at once. The contrapositive is a weapon: a functor that fails to preserve even one colimit cannot have a right adjoint, full stop. Exercise 15 uses it to prove a non-existence theorem in two lines, and §7 shows the same phenomenon in miniature: a constant map out of the divisibility order fails to preserve the initial object, and the search for its right adjoint comes back empty.

Aside · Adjoints are unique

If U has a left adjoint, it has exactly one up to natural isomorphism — the adjunction pins its partner down, just as universal properties pinned objects down in Lesson 6 (and for the same reason: §5 gives the proof, and Exercise 1 makes the connection concrete for free monoids). This is why one says the free monoid, the exponential, the product. The finder in §7’s lab returns a list of all right adjoints of a given map; in every successful run, that list has length exactly one.

§5 — Universal arrows

The third description, and the one that finds adjoints in practice

There is a third way to say “adjunction,” and it is the one that explains why adjoints are so common: an adjoint is assembled, one object at a time, from solutions to universal problems of exactly the kind studied in Lessons 6 and 7.

Fix a functor U : 𝒟 → 𝒞 and an object X of 𝒞. Lesson 5 introduced the comma category (X ↓ U): its objects are pairs (Y, f) with f : X → U Y, and an arrow from (Y, f) to (Y′, f′) is an arrow k : Y → Y′ of 𝒟 with U k ∘ f = f′.

Definition · Universal arrow

A universal arrow from X to U is an initial object of (X ↓ U): an object F X of 𝒟 with an arrow ηX : X → U F X such that every arrow f : X → U Y factors as

f = U(f̂) ∘ ηX     for exactly one f̂ : F X → Y.

This is the property of the free monoid from §1, word for word: ηX sends each generator to a one-letter list, and every function f from X into a monoid extends to exactly one homomorphism f̂. It is also the floor and ceiling of Lesson 5: a universal arrow from a real number r to the inclusion ℤ → ℝ is the least integer n with r ≤ n — the ceiling of r.

Theorem · Adjoints from universal arrows

A functor U : 𝒟 → 𝒞 has a left adjoint if and only if there is a universal arrow from X to U for every object X of 𝒞. The left adjoint sends X to the object F X of its universal arrow, and the universal arrows ηX are the components of the unit.

Proof. From an adjunction to universal arrows. Let F ⊣ U with unit η. By §4 the transpose of an arrow k : F X → Y is U k ∘ ηX. Transposition is a bijection, so every f : X → U Y is U k ∘ ηX for exactly one k. That is the statement that ηX is universal.

From universal arrows to an adjunction. Suppose a universal arrow ηX : X → U F X has been chosen for each X. So far F is defined only on objects. For an arrow h : X → X′, the composite ηX′ ∘ h is an arrow from X to U F X′, so by universality of ηX it factors through a unique arrow F X → F X′; call that arrow F h. It is characterised by

U(F h) ∘ ηX = ηX′ ∘ h.

Uniqueness makes F a functor: idF X satisfies the equation that characterises F(idX), and F h′ ∘ F h satisfies the one that characterises F(h′ ∘ h). The displayed equation is then exactly the naturality of η. Finally, the function sending k : F X → Y to U k ∘ ηX is a bijection from 𝒟(F X, Y) to 𝒞(X, U Y) — that is what universality says. It is natural in Y because U preserves composition, and natural in X by the displayed equation. So F ⊣ U. ∎

Two things follow at once.

Adjoints are built pointwise. To construct a left adjoint one does not need to produce a functor. It is enough to solve, for each object X separately, the problem “find the best arrow from X into something of the form U Y.” Functoriality and naturality then come for free. This is how free groups, free vector spaces, completions and quotients are actually constructed.

Corollary · Adjoints are unique

If F and F′ are both left adjoint to U, then F ≅ F′ by a natural isomorphism.

Proof. For each X, both ηX : X → U F X and η′X : X → U F′ X are initial objects of (X ↓ U). Initial objects are unique up to a unique isomorphism (Lesson 6), so there is exactly one isomorphism θX : F X → F′ X with U θX ∘ ηX = η′X. Naturality of θ follows from uniqueness once more: for h : X → X′, the two arrows θX′ ∘ F h and F′ h ∘ θX both factor η′X′ ∘ h through ηX. ∎

Dually, a right adjoint is assembled from universal arrows from F to Y — terminal objects of the comma categories (F ↓ Y) — and the counit components εY : F U Y → Y are those universal arrows. The mediating arrow of every universal property in Lessons 6 and 7 was a transpose in disguise.

§6 — Why right adjoints preserve limits

The theorem of §4, proved twice

§4 stated the most useful single fact about adjunctions without proof. Here are two proofs. The first is a direct diagram chase, and shows exactly where naturality is used. The second is three lines long, and shows why the theorem is true.

Theorem · RAPL, again

Let F ⊣ U, with U : 𝒟 → 𝒞. If (L, λ) is a limit of a diagram D : 𝒥 → 𝒟, then (U L, U λ) is a limit of U ∘ D.

First proof. Write f ↦ f̂ for the transposition from 𝒞(X, U Y) to 𝒟(F X, Y). Naturality in Y says that for any k : Y → Y′, the transpose of U k ∘ f is k ∘ f̂.

The legs U λj form a cone over U ∘ D, since functors preserve commuting triangles. Let (X, x) be any cone over U ∘ D in 𝒞: arrows xj : X → U D(j) with U D(a) ∘ xi = xj for every arrow a : i → j of the shape. Transpose every leg. The arrows x̂j : F X → D(j) form a cone over D, because the transpose of U D(a) ∘ xi is D(a) ∘ x̂i, and the transpose of xj is x̂j. Since L is a limit, there is exactly one n : F X → L with λj ∘ n = x̂j for all j. Let m : X → U L be the arrow whose transpose is n. Then the transpose of U λj ∘ m is λj ∘ n = x̂j, and transposition is a bijection, so U λj ∘ m = xj: the cone (X, x) factors through (U L, U λ) by m.

For uniqueness, if m′ also satisfies U λj ∘ m′ = xj, then its transpose n′ satisfies λj ∘ n′ = x̂j, so n′ = n and m′ = m. ∎

Second proof. By Lesson 7, an arrow into a limit is the same thing as a cone — hom functors preserve limits. So for every object X of 𝒞,

𝒞(X, U L) ≅ 𝒟(F X, L) ≅ lim 𝒟(F X, D(j)) ≅ lim 𝒞(X, U D(j)).

The outer two steps are the adjunction; the middle step is Lesson 7. The right-hand side is the set of cones over U ∘ D with apex X. So arrows X → U L correspond to cones from X — and that is the universal property of the limit of U ∘ D. (That a natural bijection of this kind is enough to identify U L as the limit is an instance of the Yoneda lemma, the subject of Lesson 9.) ∎

Read the second proof again: an adjunction moves the functor U across the comma of a hom-set, where it becomes F applied to the other variable — and in that variable nothing needs to be preserved. That is the whole mechanism. By duality, left adjoints preserve colimits.

A consequence for monomorphisms

Lesson 1 promised that adjunctions would have something to say about monomorphisms. Lesson 7 showed that an arrow m is monic exactly when a certain square — m against itself, with identities on the other two sides — is a pullback. A right adjoint preserves pullbacks and identities, so it carries that square to a pullback square of the same form.

Corollary · Right adjoints preserve monomorphisms

If U is a right adjoint and m is monic, then U m is monic. Dually, left adjoints preserve epimorphisms.

Lesson 2 warned that functors in general preserve neither. The forgetful functor Mon → Set is a right adjoint, so it preserves monomorphisms: a monic homomorphism of monoids is an injective function. But it does not preserve epimorphisms — the inclusion ℕ → ℤ of Lesson 1 is epic in Mon and not surjective — and the theorem tells us what that means: this forgetful functor cannot be a left adjoint. One counterexample from Lesson 1, read through RAPL, proves that no functor Set → Mon is right adjoint to “underlying set.”

§7 — Adjunctions by hand

Transposes, triangles and adjoints, on paper

Every idea in this lesson can be checked on a small example with a pencil. Four computations.

Transposing a table

Let X = {0, 1}, A = {0, 1} and Y = {0, 1, 2}, and let g : X × A → Y be addition, g(x, a) = x + a. As a table, with a row for each x and a column for each a:

g(x, a)a = 0a = 1
x = 001
x = 112

The transpose curry(g) : X → YA sends each x to its row: curry(g)(0) is the function a ↦ a, and curry(g)(1) is the function a ↦ 1 + a. Nothing has been computed. The same four numbers have been read in a different way — as one function of two arguments, or as a function whose values are functions. That is why the correspondence is a bijection, and why both sides can be counted: there are 34 = 81 ways to fill in the table, and 92 = 81 ways to choose a row for each x from the 32 = 9 possible rows.

The counit of this adjunction is evaluation, eval(k, a) = k(a): look up column a in the row k. The unit sends x to the function a ↦ (x, a), “pair with x.”

The triangle identities for lists

In the free-monoid adjunction the unit is ηX(x) = [x], and the counit εM : List(U M) → M multiplies out a list of elements of M. Take X = {a, b}.

First triangle, at the word [a, b, a]. Applying List(ηX) wraps every letter: the result is [[a], [b], [a]], a list of three one-letter words. The counit of the monoid List X multiplies these out, and multiplication in List X is concatenation: [a] ⧺ [b] ⧺ [a] = [a, b, a]. We are back where we started.

Second triangle, at an element m of a monoid M. The unit of the set U M wraps it as the one-element list [m], and the counit multiplies out a product with a single factor: m.

So for lists the triangle identities say that a word is the concatenation of its letters, and that a product with one factor is that factor. Units, counits and triangle identities usually decode to something this modest (Exercise 10) — the content of an adjunction is in the bijection they generate.

Finding an adjoint in an order

Between orders the universal arrows of §5 turn into a recipe. If a monotone map f has a right adjoint g, then f(x) ≤ y exactly when x ≤ g(y), so g(y) is forced:

g(y) = the greatest x with f(x) ≤ y.

Conversely, if f is monotone and such a greatest x exists for every y, these values define a right adjoint: the biconditional holds, and by Exercise 4 the map g is then automatically monotone. Dually, a left adjoint must send y to the least x with y ≤ f(x).

Try it on the map f from the chain 0 ≤ 1 ≤ 2 to the divisibility order on {1, 2, 3, 6} with f(0) = 1, f(1) = 2, f(2) = 6.

So f has a right adjoint, g(1) = 0, g(2) = 1, g(3) = 0, g(6) = 2: the largest element of the chain whose image divides y, a “round down.” One instance of the biconditional, to see it at work: f(1) = 2 divides 6, and correspondingly 1 ≤ g(6) = 2; but f(1) = 2 does not divide 3, and correspondingly 1 ≤ g(3) = 0 is false.

Now a failure. Let c be the constant map from the divisibility order to the chain with c(x) = 1 for all x. A right adjoint would have to send 0 to the greatest x with c(x) ≤ 0. There is no such x at all: the set is empty, and an empty set has no greatest element. So c has no right adjoint. The dual of RAPL predicted it: a left adjoint preserves initial objects, and c sends the least element 1 of the divisibility order to 1, which is not the least element of the chain.

Second, the adjunction that opened the lesson, as running code. fold_map constructs the transpose f̂ from f; we then check the three claims the adjunction makes about it — it is a homomorphism (empty list to unit, concatenation to the operation), and composing with the unit η(x) = [x] recovers f exactly:

free.py — foldMap is the transpose
def fold_map(f, unit, op):
    "the unique monoid hom  List X -> M  extending  f : X -> M"
    def hat(xs):
        acc = unit
        for x in xs:
            acc = op(acc, f(x))
        return acc
    return hat

f   = {'a': 2, 'b': 3}.get
hat = fold_map(f, 0, lambda m, n: m + n)      # target monoid (Z, +, 0)

import itertools

Third, the finder. Between finite posets — Lesson 6’s divisibility order and a three-element chain, built as FiniteCategory objects — an adjunction is the Galois biconditional, so the set of all right adjoints of a given monotone map is computable by brute force, in the style of Lessons 6 and 7. First the two orders, with the category class carried over from Lesson 1:

setup.py — the category class and two orders
from itertools import product

class FiniteCategory:
    """objects, arrows (name -> (dom, cod)), a composition table,
       and a chosen identity arrow for each object."""
    def __init__(self, objects, arrows, comp, ident):
        self.obj, self.ar = set(objects), arrows
        self.comp, self.id = comp, ident

    def dom(self, a): return self.ar[a][0]
    def cod(self, a): return self.ar[a][1]
    def composable(self, g, f): return self.dom(g) == self.cod(f)

    def check(self):
        # 1. each identity is typed X -> X
        for X in self.obj:
            assert self.ar[self.id[X]] == (X, X)
        # 2. every composable pair has a composite, correctly typed
        for g, f in product(self.ar, repeat=2):
            if self.composable(g, f):
                gf = self.comp[(g, f)]
                assert self.ar[gf] == (self.dom(f), self.cod(g))
        # 3. identity law on both sides
        for f in self.ar:
            X, Y = self.ar[f]
            assert self.comp[(self.id[Y], f)] == f
            assert self.comp[(f, self.id[X])] == f
        # 4. associativity
        for h, g, f in product(self.ar, repeat=3):
            if self.composable(h, g) and self.composable(g, f):
                assert self.comp[(h, self.comp[(g, f)])] == \
                       self.comp[(self.comp[(h, g)], f)]
        return True

def arrows_between(C, X, Y):
    "all arrows X -> Y of the finite category C"
    return [a for a, (d, c) in C.ar.items() if d == X and c == Y]

def make_poset(objs, leq):
    "a finite preorder as a thin category; the arrow a -> b is named 'a<=b'"
    ar = {f"{a}<={b}": (a, b) for a in objs for b in objs if leq(a, b)}
    comp = {(g, f): f"{ar[f][0]}<={ar[g][1]}"
            for g in ar for f in ar if ar[f][1] == ar[g][0]}
    return FiniteCategory(objs, ar, comp, {a: f"{a}<={a}" for a in objs})

Going deeper

Quantifiers are adjoints

The most startling entry in the adjunction gallery was left for last. Fix a function f : A → B and consider the two powersets P(A) and P(B), each a poset under inclusion. Preimage f* : P(B) → P(A) is monotone — and it is flanked by two adjoints, both of which every mathematician already uses daily:

∃f ⊣ f* ⊣ ∀f

where ∃f(S) = { f(x) : x ∈ S } is the direct image (“some preimage lies in S”) and ∀f(S) = { b : f−1(b) ⊆ S } (“every preimage lies in S”). The two Galois biconditionals — ∃f(S) ⊆ T ⟺ S ⊆ f*(T) and f*(T) ⊆ S ⟺ T ⊆ ∀f(S) — are, read logically, the introduction and elimination rules for the quantifiers. This observation (Lawvere’s, from the 1960s) is a doorway into categorical logic: the logical quantifiers are not primitive; they are the adjoints of substitution. Exercise 18 verifies both adjunctions exhaustively; the sandwich shape — a right adjoint that is itself a left adjoint — is the ceiling/inclusion/floor pattern again, one floor up.

When does an adjoint exist?

RAPL gives a necessary condition: a functor with a left adjoint must preserve limits. How far is that from being sufficient? Between orders that have all meets and joins — complete lattices, such as a power set — it is the whole story.

Theorem · The adjoint functor theorem for orders

Let f : P → Q be a monotone map between complete lattices. Then f has a right adjoint if and only if it preserves all joins, and a left adjoint if and only if it preserves all meets.

Proof. If f has a right adjoint it is a left adjoint, and preserves all colimits — in an order, all joins. Conversely, suppose f preserves joins, and define g(y) to be the join of the set S of all x with f(x) ≤ y. Then f(g(y)) is the join of the elements f(x) for x in S, each of which is ≤ y; so f(g(y)) ≤ y. That makes g(y) an element of S, hence its greatest element, and §7 showed that this is what a right adjoint needs. The other half is dual. ∎

The proof is the recipe of §7 with one addition: the candidate is always available — take the join of everything that should lie below g(y) — and preservation of joins is exactly what is needed for it to work. For general categories the same idea yields Freyd’s adjoint functor theorems: a functor between suitably complete categories has a left adjoint if and only if it preserves limits and satisfies a further condition, which keeps the construction from involving too large a collection of objects. RAPL is the easy half of a statement whose converse is very nearly true, and that is why “does it preserve limits?” is the first question to ask of any functor.

The quantifiers above are an instance. Preimage f* : P(B) → P(A) preserves all unions and all intersections, so the theorem hands it both adjoints at once — and its formula for the right adjoint, “the union of all T with f*(T) ⊆ S,” is ∀f(S).

Show solution

(a) f̂([a, b, a]) = f(a) + f(b) + f(a) = 2 + 3 + 2 = 7 — the value that the lab in §7 prints.

(b) Any list factors as a concatenation of singletons: [x₁, …, xₙ] = [x₁] ⧺ ⋯ ⧺ [xₙ]. Operation-preservation handled every list of length ≥ 1; unit-preservation handled length 0; no room was left anywhere.

This is what “no relations beyond the forced ones” buys: the generators generate, so agreement on generators plus the homomorphism laws leaves zero freedom. Show solution

First round trip. φ(ψ(f)) = U(εY ∘ F f) ∘ ηX = U εY ∘ U F f ∘ ηX (functoriality of U). Now the naturality square of η at the arrow f : X → U Y says U F f ∘ ηX = ηU Y ∘ f — slide it in: = U εY ∘ ηU Y ∘ f. The second triangle identity, at the object Y, says U εY ∘ ηU Y = idU Y. Hence φ(ψ(f)) = f.

Second round trip. ψ(φ(f̂)) = εY ∘ F(U f̂ ∘ ηX) = εY ∘ F U f̂ ∘ F ηX. Naturality of ε at the arrow f̂ : F X → Y gives εY ∘ F U f̂ = f̂ ∘ εF X: = f̂ ∘ εF X ∘ F ηX, and the first triangle identity at X collapses the tail to idF X. Hence ψ(φ(f̂)) = f̂.

Every tool in the proof was built in an earlier lesson: functor laws (Lesson 2), naturality squares (Lesson 3), and a pair of equations playing exactly the role the “exactly one” clause played in Lessons 6–7. This exercise is the course’s machinery operating as a single machine.

Exercise 13arrows-only

The two definitions agree. Exercise 12 showed that the transpositions built from a unit and a counit are mutually inverse. Complete the equivalence between the definition of §2 and the definition of §4.

  1. Start from a bijection φ from 𝒟(F X, Y) to 𝒞(X, U Y), natural in both variables as spelled out in §2, and put ηX = φ(idF X). Show that φ(k) = U k ∘ ηX for every k : F X → Y, and that η is a natural transformation.
  2. Let ψ be the inverse of φ and εY = ψ(idU Y). Granting the dual formula ψ(f) = εY ∘ F f, derive both triangle identities.
  3. Conversely, start from natural transformations η : Id ⇒ U ∘ F and ε : F ∘ U ⇒ Id and define φ(k) = U k ∘ ηX. Show that φ is natural in both variables.
Show solution

(a) Naturality in Y, applied to idF X and the arrow k : F X → Y, reads φ(k ∘ idF X) = U k ∘ φ(idF X); that is, φ(k) = U k ∘ ηX. For the naturality of η, take h : X′ → X and compute φ(F h) in two ways. By the formula just proved, used at X′, it is U F h ∘ ηX′. By naturality in X, applied to idF X, it is φ(idF X ∘ F h) = ηX ∘ h. So U F h ∘ ηX′ = ηX ∘ h, which is the naturality square of η at h.

(b) εF X ∘ F ηX = ψ(ηX) = ψ(φ(idF X)) = idF X, and U εY ∘ ηU Y = φ(εY) = φ(ψ(idU Y)) = idU Y. Each triangle identity says that a unit or counit, transposed back, is the identity it came from.

(c) Natural in Y: for k′ : Y → Y′, φ(k′ ∘ k) = U(k′ ∘ k) ∘ ηX = U k′ ∘ U k ∘ ηX = U k′ ∘ φ(k), because U preserves composition. Natural in X: for h : X′ → X, φ(k ∘ F h) = U k ∘ U F h ∘ ηX′ = U k ∘ ηX ∘ h = φ(k) ∘ h, by the naturality square of η at h.

Notice the division of labour. Naturality of the transposition needs only the naturality of η; the triangle identities are needed for exactly one thing, that the two transpositions are mutually inverse (Exercise 12). Nothing is lost in either translation, so the two definitions describe the same structure.

Exercise 14arrows-only

When is a right adjoint faithful? Let F ⊣ U with counit ε.

  1. Show that for every arrow k : Y → Y′ of 𝒟, the arrow F U Y → Y′ corresponding to U k : U Y → U Y′ under the adjunction is k ∘ εY.
  2. Deduce that U is faithful if and only if every component εY is an epimorphism.
  3. What does (b) say about monoids?
Show solution

(a) By §4, the arrow corresponding to f : X → U Y′ is εY′ ∘ F f. Take X = U Y and f = U k: the result is εY′ ∘ F U k, which equals k ∘ εY by the naturality square of ε at k.

(b) U is faithful when, for all Y and Y′, the function k ↦ U k from 𝒟(Y, Y′) to 𝒞(U Y, U Y′) is injective. Following it by the bijection 𝒞(U Y, U Y′) ≅ 𝒟(F U Y, Y′) does not affect injectivity, and by (a) the composite is k ↦ k ∘ εY. So U is faithful exactly when k ∘ εY = k′ ∘ εY implies k = k′, for all parallel k, k′ out of Y — which is the definition of εY being epic.

(c) The forgetful functor Mon → Set is faithful, so every counit εM : List(U M) → M is an epimorphism. Here one can see more: εM is surjective, since each element m is the image of the one-letter word [m]. So every monoid is a quotient of a free monoid, and the same argument works for groups, rings and vector spaces. This is the starting point for describing an algebraic structure by generators and relations.

Dually, F is faithful if and only if every unit component ηX is monic. For lists, ηX(x) = [x] is injective.

Adjoints and limits

Exercise 15think

A two-line non-existence theorem. Using RAPL’s dual — left adjoints preserve colimits, in particular initial objects — prove that the forgetful functor U : Grp → Set has no right adjoint.

(You will need Lesson 6’s inventory: the initial object of Grp is the trivial group; the initial object of Set is the empty set.)

Show solution

If U had a right adjoint, U would be a left adjoint, and would therefore preserve initial objects. But U sends the initial object of Grp — the trivial group — to a one-element set, and the initial object of Set is the empty set. One-element sets are not initial (they receive many maps, and Lesson 6, Exercise 1 counted them), so U does not preserve the initial object, and no right adjoint exists. ∎

Notice the asymmetry with the other flank: U does have a left adjoint (free groups), which is why it preserves all limits — products, kernels-as-equalizers, pullbacks. The two adjoint questions about a single functor are genuinely independent, and RAPL turns each into a checklist of concrete preservation tests. The constant map of §7 is this exact argument in a seven-element universe.

Exercise 16think

Arithmetic from RAPL. All functors here go from Set to Set, and A is a fixed set.

  1. (−) × A is a left adjoint, so it preserves coproducts and the initial object. Which two laws of arithmetic result, when the sets are finite and you count?
  2. (−)A is a right adjoint, so it preserves products and the terminal object. Which two laws result?
  3. Let A be non-empty. Show that the functor (−) + A has neither a left adjoint nor a right adjoint.
  4. For which sets A does (−) × A have a left adjoint?
Show solution

(a) (B + C) × A ≅ B × A + C × A and ∅ × A ≅ ∅: the distributive law (b + c) · a = b · a + c · a, and 0 · a = 0.

(b) (Y × Z)A ≅ YA × ZA and 1A ≅ 1: the laws (y · z)a = ya · za and 1a = 1.

(c) If (−) + A had a right adjoint, it would be a left adjoint and would preserve the initial object; but ∅ + A = A is not empty. If it had a left adjoint, it would be a right adjoint and would preserve the terminal object; but 1 + A has at least two elements. So it has neither.

(d) A functor with a left adjoint is a right adjoint, and must preserve the terminal object. 1 × A ≅ A is terminal only when A has exactly one element. In that case (−) × A is naturally isomorphic to the identity functor, which is its own adjoint on both sides. So: only for one-element sets.

Every familiar identity relating sums, products and powers of natural numbers is the shadow of a preservation statement of this kind. One of them, (y · z)a = ya · za, was already met in Lesson 6, where it came directly from the universal property of the product.

Exercise 17structure

Four adjoints in a row. Let Pre be the category of preorders and monotone maps, and U : Pre → Set the underlying-set functor. For a set S, let D S be S with the discrete order, in which x ≤ y only when x = y, and let I S be S with the indiscrete order, in which x ≤ y always.

  1. Show that D ⊣ U ⊣ I by describing the bijections Pre(D S, P) ≅ Set(S, U P) and Set(U P, S) ≅ Pre(P, I S).
  2. What does RAPL say about U? Check it against the product and the coproduct of two preorders.
  3. Call two elements of a preorder connected if they are joined by a zigzag x ≤ y ≥ z ≤ ⋯, and let π0(P) be the set of connected components of P. Show that π0 ⊣ D.
  4. Show that I has no right adjoint.
Show solution

(a) A function out of a discrete order is automatically monotone: the condition “x ≤ y implies f(x) ≤ f(y)” only has to be checked when x = y. So monotone maps D S → P are the same thing as functions S → U P. A function into an indiscrete order is automatically monotone, since f(x) ≤ f(y) always holds; so monotone maps P → I S are the same thing as functions U P → S. Both bijections are the identity on underlying functions, so naturality is immediate.

(b) U is a right adjoint (of D) and also a left adjoint (of I), so it preserves all limits and all colimits. And indeed the product of P and Q is the product of the underlying sets, ordered componentwise; their coproduct is the disjoint union of the underlying sets, with no relations between the two parts.

(c) A monotone map f : P → D S must satisfy f(x) = f(y) whenever x ≤ y. So it is constant along zigzags — constant on each connected component — and conversely every function that is constant on components is monotone into D S. Such functions are the same thing as functions π0(P) → S. That is a bijection Set(π0 P, S) ≅ Pre(P, D S), natural in both variables.

(d) If I had a right adjoint it would be a left adjoint, and would preserve coproducts. But for non-empty sets S and T, in I(S + T) every element of S is below every element of T, while in the coproduct I S + I T no element of one part is related to any element of the other. So I does not preserve coproducts.

The result is a chain π0 ⊣ D ⊣ U ⊣ I: one humble functor, “underlying set,” with an adjoint on each side, and one of those with a further adjoint of its own. The same chain appears with categories in place of preorders: connected components, discrete category, set of objects, and the category with exactly one arrow between any two objects. For topological spaces three of the four survive in general — discrete space, underlying set of points, indiscrete space.

An element with empty preimage satisfies “every preimage lies in S” vacuously, so it belongs to ∀f(S) for every S — the code’s all(...) over an empty iterable returns True, which is the right answer for the same vacuous reason the empty function made ∅ initial in Lesson 6.

quantifiers.py
from itertools import combinations

A = {1, 2, 3}; B = {'a', 'b'}
fpt = {1: 'a', 2: 'a', 3: 'b'}
f = lambda x: fpt[x]

def subsets(S):
    S = list(S)
    return [set(c) for r in range(len(S)+1) for c in combinations(S, r)]

E   = lambda S: {f(x) for x in S}                     # ∃f : direct image
pre = lambda T: {x for x in A if f(x) in T}           # f* : preimage
All = lambda S: {b for b in B                         # ∀f
                 if all(x in S for x in A if f(x) == b)}

n = 0
for S in subsets(A):
    for T in subsets(B):
        assert (E(S) <= T) == (S <= pre(T))     # ∃f  -|  f*
        assert (pre(T) <= S) == (T <= All(S))   # f*  -|  ∀f
        n += 2
print(f"quantifier sandwich verified on {n} biconditionals")

Closure. Let f ⊣ g be a Galois connection, with f : P → Q and g : Q → P monotone maps between posets, and put c = g ∘ f.

  2. Deduce that c is monotone, that x ≤ c(x), and that c(c(x)) = c(x).
  3. For the adjunction ∃f ⊣ f* of the Going-deeper section, describe c(S) = f*(∃f(S)) in words, and compute it for the function of Exercise 18 and S = {1}.
  4. An operation with the three properties of (b) is called a closure operator. Name two that you have met elsewhere in mathematics.
Show solution

(a) Apply f to the unit inequality x ≤ g(f(x)) of Exercise 4: f(x) ≤ f(g(f(x))). The counit inequality at y = f(x) gives f(g(f(x))) ≤ f(x). In a poset, two elements each below the other are equal. This is the first triangle identity, εF X ∘ F ηX = idF X, in a setting where an equation between arrows is just an equation between their endpoints.

(b) c is a composite of monotone maps (Exercise 4). x ≤ c(x) is the unit. And applying g to (a) gives g(f(g(f(x)))) = g(f(x)), that is, c(c(x)) = c(x).

(c) c(S) is the set of all elements of A that have the same image as some element of S: the union of the fibres of f that meet S. For f(1) = f(2) = a, f(3) = b and S = {1}: ∃f(S) = {a} and f*({a}) = {1, 2}.

(d) The subgroup generated by a subset of a group; the span of a set of vectors; the closure of a set in a topological space; the convex hull of a set of points; the transitive closure of a relation. Each comes from an adjunction of this kind — for instance, “subgroup generated by” is left adjoint to the inclusion of the subgroups of G into the subsets of G.

A closure operator is what a monad looks like in a poset: x ≤ c(x) is its unit and c(c(x)) ≤ c(x) its multiplication. Every adjunction produces one, in any category, and Lesson 10 is about what they are and what they are for.

Recap

What to carry forward

  1. An adjunction F ⊣ U is a bijection 𝒟(F X, Y) ≅ 𝒞(X, U Y), natural in both variables — a perfect exchange rate between two kinds of arrow. The left adjoint sits on the left of the hom-set.
  2. Equivalently: a unit η : Id ⇒ UF and counit ε : FU ⇒ Id satisfying the two triangle identities. Transposition is U(−) ∘ η one way and ε ∘ F(−) the other.
  3. The gallery: free ⊣ forgetful (foldMap), currying (−) × A ⊣ (−)A with eval as counit, Galois connections in posets (⌈−⌉ ⊣ i ⊣ ⌊−⌋), and + ⊣ Δ ⊣ × — limits and colimits are adjoints of diagonal functors.
  4. Universal arrows: U has a left adjoint exactly when every comma category (X ↓ U) has an initial object. Adjoints are built one object at a time, and are unique up to natural isomorphism.
  5. RAPL: right adjoints preserve limits, left adjoints preserve colimits — because an arrow into a limit is a cone, and the adjunction moves U to the other side of the hom-set. The contrapositive proves the non-existence of adjoints in two lines.
  6. Between orders an adjunction is a Galois connection, and the adjoint can be computed: g(y) is the greatest x with f(x) ≤ y. In finite posets the search always returns one adjoint or none.
